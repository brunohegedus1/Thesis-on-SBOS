\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Summary}
\markboth{Summary}{Summary}

Around 2000, Background Oriented Schlieren proposed to replace the large optical elements used by traditional schlieren with a background pattern, digital optical sensors and the use of cross-correlation algorithms \cite{Dalziel2000}. The cost was an inherent tie between the sensitivity and the physical distances in the optical setup, which led to a design compromise between sensitivity and resolution \cite{Raffel2015, Schmidt2025}. By replacing the printed background pattern with a laser-speckled pattern that constraint is relaxed \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. Since the speckle is always in-focus, it can be positioned anywhere in the line of sight of the camera, allowing for unusual focus positions, namely in front of or inside of it. The resulting technique, Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren, remains relatively unexplored compared to standard BOS and lacks a coherent design framework for its implementation. This thesis investigates the application of the method to a supersonic wind tunnel, through four experimental campaigns that address each of the three components of the method: the optical configuration, the speckle generator and the refractive object. The last of these campaigns brings the three together in a high-speed wind tunnel.

A design methodology was first developed to compute the performance parameters of the optical setup from its physical variables and to size the experimental setups. It identified the variables that govern the sensitivity-resolution ratio and showed how a negative defocus configuration improves it compared to standard BOS. Surface maps of the design space revealed the main trends, which were turned into design guidelines together with a spatial resolution criterion. An angled glass plate that induced a known displacement served to validate the geometric predictions of the methodology and to compare different in-focus positions: the measured magnifications matched the design within 5\% and the measured displacements matched the prediction within 4\%, each at all but one point. Uncertainty budgets were built for the predicted and the measured displacements. A dedicated campaign on the speckle pattern showed that the average speckle size can be controlled through the f-stop, although it grows more weakly than predicted by the Rayleigh diffraction limit, and that it is insensitive to the optical magnification. It nevertheless led to a speckle generator capable of producing speckles between 2 and 5 pixels. An under-expanded compressible jet was then imaged, and its displacement field was converted into a quantitative density field through Abel inversion with Tikhonov regularization \cite{Daun2006}. The measured shock cell lengths are consistent with the correlations of Hartmann and Lazarus \cite{Hartmann1941}, and the centerline density matches that of Panda and Seasholtz \cite{Panda1999} over the first shock cell.

The final experimental campaign applied the method to the flow of a linear aerospike nozzle in TU Delft's ST-15 supersonic wind tunnel. With the focus inside the test section, the usual model $S = l_{eff} M_{ag}$ no longer holds, so a method was introduced that recovers the sensitivity from the ratio of displacements measured at two configurations; where it could be checked, it agreed with the geometric sensitivity within 5\%. In-focus SBOS identified the same compression waves, expansion fans and shear layer as schlieren images of the same flow, approaching their resolution, whereas the defocused configurations blurred the pattern to decorrelation under strong density gradients. Taken together, the four campaigns propose a design route for SBOS setups and show that the method can be applied in a high-speed wind tunnel with a simple optical arrangement and a low-power laser.
