\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Summary}
\markboth{Summary}{Summary}

Around 2000, Background Oriented Schlieren proposed to replace the large optical elements used by traditional schlieren with a background pattern and the use of cross-correlation algorithms \cite{Dalziel2000, Raffel2001}. The cost was an inherent tie between the sensitivity and the physical distances in the optical setup, which led to a design compromise between sensitivity and resolution \cite{Raffel2015, Schmidt2025}. Replacing the printed background pattern with a laser-speckled pattern relaxes that constraint \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. Since the speckle pattern is always in-focus, the background can be positioned anywhere in the line of sight of the camera, allowing for unusual reference positions, namely in front of or inside of the test section. The resulting technique, Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren, remains relatively unexplored compared to standard BOS and lacks a coherent design framework for its implementation. This thesis investigates the application of the method to a supersonic wind tunnel, through four experimental campaigns that address each of the three components of the method: the optical configuration, the speckle generation and the refractive object. The last of these campaigns brings the three together in a high-speed wind tunnel.

A design methodology was first developed to compute the performance parameters of the optical setup from its physical variables, aiming to size the experimental setups. It identified the variables that govern the sensitivity-resolution ratio and showed the advantage of negative defocus compared to standard BOS in improving it. Surface maps of the design space revealed the main design trends, which were turned into guidelines that allowed the definition of an experimental setup with the desired performance. An angled glass plate that induced a known displacement served to validate the geometric predictions of the methodology and to compare different in-focus positions: the measured magnifications matched the design within 5\% and the measured displacements matched the prediction within 4\%. Uncertainty budgets were built for the predicted and the measured displacements to better understand the deviations. Later, a dedicated campaign on the speckle pattern showed that the average speckle size can be controlled through the f-stop, although it is insensitive to the optical magnification. It nevertheless led to a speckle generator capable of producing speckles between 2 and 5 pixels, the optimal size for cross-correlation algorithms \cite{KahlerEtAl}. Then, the method was implemented to image a real refractive object, an under-expanded compressible jet, and its displacement field was converted into a quantitative density field through Abel inversion with Tikhonov regularization \cite{Daun2006}. Compared the results with literature, the measured shock cell lengths are consistent with the correlations of Hartmann and Lazarus \cite{Hartmann1941}, and the centerline density matches that of Panda and Seasholtz \cite{Panda1999} over the first shock cell.

The final experimental campaign applied the method to the flow of a linear aerospike nozzle in TU Delft's ST-15 supersonic wind tunnel. Different defocus configurations were tested, including positioning the background inside the test-section. With the reference inside the test section, the usual model $S = l_{eff} M_{ag}$ no longer holds, so an strategy was envisioned to recover the sensitivity from the ratio of displacements measured at two defocus configurations. Where it could be checked, the results agreed with the geometric sensitivity within 5\%. In-focus SBOS identified the same compression waves, expansion fans and shear layer as schlieren images of the same flow, approaching their resolution. While the defocused configurations blurred the pattern to decorrelation under strong density gradients. Taken together, the four campaigns propose a design route for SBOS setups and show that the technique can be applied in a high-speed wind tunnel with a simple optical arrangement and a low-power laser.
