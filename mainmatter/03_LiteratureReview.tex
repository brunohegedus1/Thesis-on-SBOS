\chapter{Optical Density Measurement Techniques}
\label{chapter: LiteratureReview}

\par Several techniques have been developed which use light refraction as a principle to visualize changes in density. These methods can be grouped into classical techniques, such as shadowgraphy, schlieren and interferometry, and modern methods, such as Background Oriented Schlieren and its variants. This chapter will provide a brief historical background on classical density gradient measurement techniques and explain how these methods have developed leading up to BOS. Then, a more detailed literature review is given on BOS and SBOS to better define the state of the art of these methods and provide context to the research gap that this thesis aims to fill.


\section{Classical Methods}
\label{section: Classical Methods}

\par The earliest report of a density visualization technique dates back to the 1600s, when Hooke saw the flow around a candle flame with the aid of a lens \cite{Settles2001}. His experiments resemble setups used for shadowgraphy, where a test subject is positioned in between an observer and a light source, as shown in Figure \ref{fig: Hooke shadowgraphy}. The variations in the refractive index at the test section bend the light coming from the source, producing shadows and brighter regions captured by the observer. Valued for its simplicity, shadowgraphy continues to be an important flow visualization technique, complementing more complex methods in the study of compressible flows \cite{Settles2017}. However, it provides mainly qualitative results, as quantitative reconstruction is difficult due to its dependence on the second spatial derivative of density. This motivated the development of techniques that depend on the first spatial derivative of density, such as schlieren.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/Hooke shadowgraphy setup.png}
    \caption[Hooke's shadowgraphy setup]{Hooke's shadowgraphy setup, credit to Settles \cite{Settles2001}}
    \label{fig: Hooke shadowgraphy}
\end{figure}

\par In the 1860s, Toepler developed the method that he named after the German word \emph{Schlieren} (streaks), and was the first to apply it as a scientific tool \cite{Settles2001}. Toepler's setup used a lens to focus an image of the light source, where a knife edge was placed to block part of it. The knife edge works as a spatial filter: the light coming from the test section deflects towards or away from the edge, producing brighter and darker regions in the image. As a result, the technique is more sensitive to deviations perpendicular to the knife edge and depends on the first derivative of density in that direction. A simple schlieren setup can be seen in Figure \ref{fig: Schlieren setup}. The technique provides high spatial resolution and sensitivity to small density gradients, which led it to be extensively used throughout the years for the research of compressible flows \cite{Settles2001}. The use of schlieren continues to be relevant to this day and many variants of the method exist, providing unique advantages and applications \cite{Settles2017}. However, conventional schlieren needs large, costly optical elements for larger fields of view, with a straight knife edge it is only sensitive to density gradients in one direction, and the reconstruction of quantitative density fields from schlieren images is not straightforward, since the image intensity must be calibrated against the deflection.

    \caption[Simple schlieren setup]{Illustration of a simple schlieren setup}
    \label{fig: Schlieren setup}
\end{figure}

\par Laser interferometry is another optical method used to study refractive objects. In this technique, the change in optical phase of light transmitted through the fluid is measured by an instrument called an interferometer \cite{Merzkirch2007}. One example of these devices is the Mach--Zehnder interferometer. In it, a laser beam is split into two: one beam passes through the test object, whose refractive index field changes its optical path length and therefore its phase, while the other, the reference beam, bypasses it. When the two beams are recombined at the sensor, they interfere, producing interference fringes that can be related to the refractive index change in the test object. Since the fringe shift is proportional to the optical path difference, interferometry responds to the density itself, integrated along the line of sight, rather than to its first (schlieren) or second (shadowgraphy) derivative. This comes at the cost of a more complex setup \cite{Ristic2006}.

\par A related technique that measures light deflection is laser speckle photography, used for flow analysis by Debrus \cite{Debrus1972} and Köpf \cite{Kopf1972}. They used a ground glass diffuser to scatter the laser beam and produce a speckle pattern. A sensitive film is exposed to this pattern twice, with and without flow passing through the test section. The flow displaces the speckles between the two exposures, and when the double-exposed film is probed with a narrow laser beam it produces Young's fringes, whose spacing is inversely proportional to the local speckle displacement and whose orientation is perpendicular to it \cite{NikitaFominBook}. Köpf's setup can be seen in Figure \ref{fig: laser speckled interferometry}. This early use of speckle patterns anticipates their later application in SBOS configurations. Unlike conventional schlieren, the technique provides a direct measurement of the light displacement between the two states, from which both the magnitude and direction of the deflection can be obtained. The technique, however, requires a coherent light source and a two-step recording and readout process.

    \caption[Laser speckle photography setup]{Laser speckle photography setup, credit to Köpf \cite{Kopf1972}}
    \label{fig: laser speckled interferometry}
\end{figure}

\par The classical density gradient visualization methods presented and briefly discussed in this section are still relevant to this day \cite{Settles2017}. Shadowgraphy's simplicity gives quick qualitative insights into the flow; schlieren's high resolution and sensitivity motivate its use in many cases; and interferometry allows a more direct way to obtain quantitative density field measurements. However, each has its drawbacks: shadowgraphy gives mainly qualitative results; schlieren requires optics as large as the field of view and, with a knife edge, senses gradients in a single direction; interferometry demands a complex, vibration-sensitive setup; and speckle photography requires a two-step photographic recording and readout. Background Oriented Schlieren provides a simple, whole-field and scalable technique with quantitative potential. Its own limitations, however, leave room for improvement, as will be discussed in the next section.

\section{Background Oriented Schlieren}
\label{section: Background Oriented Schlieren}
\par In the year 2000, a new technique emerged taking advantage of new sensors and image processing algorithms, the Background Oriented Schlieren (BOS) technique \cite{Dalziel2000, Raffel2001}. The method consists of measuring the displacement of a background image due to the presence of a test flow. To achieve that, a background pattern is positioned after the test section, an image of the pattern is captured when no flow is present and another when there is a density gradient that displaces the background. This displacement is quantified using image sensing algorithms and can be related to the flow's density as shown in Section \ref{section: Refraction} \cite{Schmidt2025}. This method greatly simplifies the schlieren setup, provides a whole field analysis of the test section and is easier to scale than the traditional methods. These advantages, together with its non-intrusive nature and potential for quantitative measurements, have contributed to its widespread adoption among experimentalists, with many improved versions being proposed over the years. Figure \ref{fig: BOS setup Raffel} shows a typical BOS setup.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/Raffel BOS setup.png}
    \caption[Standard BOS setup]{BOS setup, credit to Raffel \cite{Raffel2001}}
    \label{fig: BOS setup Raffel}
\end{figure}

\par Practical guidelines for the design of BOS setups have been established in the literature. Both Gojani et al. \cite{Gojani&Obayashi} and Schwarz and Braukmann \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann} highlighted the importance of sensitivity and spatial resolution in defining the optical configuration for BOS. Sensitivity $S$ can be interpreted as the ability of the optical configuration to convert a measured displacement of the background pattern into light deflection, and is set by the product of the defocus distance $l$ and the optical magnification $M_{ag}$, $S = l\cdot M_{ag}$. Spatial resolution describes the smallest spatial scale of the flow that can be distinguished by the measurement system and is commonly characterized by a minimum resolvable feature size, $s_{min}$. The optical spatial resolution is strongly influenced by the blur of the refractive object, which can be characterized by the circle of confusion (CoC) \cite{Greenleaf1950}; its derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{Appendix: CoC derivation}. The complete set of relations linking these two performance parameters to the physical layout of the setup is assembled in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}, where it forms the basis of the design strategy of this thesis.

\par Sensitivity grows with the distance between the refractive object and the background, so the first instinct is to place the background as far away as possible. The returns diminish, however: as the defocus distance grows to infinity the sensitivity saturates at the focal length of the lens, $S \rightarrow f$. A longer focal length therefore increases the maximum achievable sensitivity, but $f$ is itself constrained by the desired field of view $L_{FoV_{obj}}$ and the camera sensor size $L_{sens}$ \cite{Schmidt2025}. This asymptotic behavior is examined quantitatively in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}.

\par Furthermore, there is an inherent optical focusing trade-off in BOS. During the setup assembly, one would like to focus on the background for better contrast, while positioning the background farther away from the test subject for increased sensitivity. However, as the distance between the test subject and the in-focus plane increases, the blur of the refractive object is worsened and hinders the spatial resolution of the setup. One way to reduce the blur is to increase the depth of field by increasing the f-number, $f_\#$, which increases the distance from the in-focus plane where the $CoC$ is still acceptable (see Appendix \ref{Appendix: CoC derivation} and Greenleaf \cite{Greenleaf1950}). However, reducing the aperture decreases the amount of light reaching the sensor, potentially reducing the image signal-to-noise ratio and background contrast. Consequently, a compromise must be made between spatial resolution and sensitivity, tuning the optical setup to achieve the maximum signal and acceptable blur.

\par Although it provides advantages compared to classical methods, the design of a BOS setup is marked by constraints and compromises. The achievable sensitivity is limited by the geometric configuration as increasing the sensitivity requires larger optical distances, which in turn degrade spatial resolution due to increased blur. Careful optimization of parameters is required to balance these competing effects. These limitations motivate the exploration of alternative approaches. By using Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren (SBOS) many of these shortcomings could be overcome, as will be discussed.

\par Before introducing SBOS, however, further aspects of BOS deserve attention. The first corresponds to an ambiguity found in the literature regarding the optical spatial resolution. Then the influence of the imaging hardware and post-processing is discussed, as both constrain accuracy and resolution as much as optics do. Lastly, a range of variants derived from BOS over the past years will be presented, which gives the context for understanding both the strengths and the particular niche SBOS occupies. The next subsections will treat these matters.

\subsection{Optical Spatial Resolution}
\label{subsection: Optical Spatial Resolution}
\par In optical configurations, spatial resolution is limited by the amount of blur of the object. Despite the clear conceptual definition, there is no consensus on a definitive mathematical formulation of optical spatial resolution. Historically, the concept of Circle of Confusion $CoC$ has been used to describe the blur in the image plane \cite{Greenleaf1950}; its derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{Appendix: CoC derivation}. Schwarz and Braukmann \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann} expanded on the concept: they experimentally tested various BOS setups and used a separability criterion to define where a visual target strip stopped being discernible from another. Then, they correlated that distance with the Circle of Confusion transferred to the object plane $CoC_{obj}$ and concluded that the minimum distinguishable feature $s_{min}$ equals half the $CoC$ at the object plane. Meanwhile, Gojani et al. \cite{Gojani&Obayashi} took another route and derived an equation for the blur circle in the object plane by using geometry and theoretical assumptions. The two formulations do not yield the same result, so an investigation into the methods used to obtain them was conducted. Both equations are summarized in Table \ref{tab: SR formulations}.

    \label{tab: SR formulations}
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \toprule
        Formulation & Expression \\

    \end{tabular}

\par The two formulations on the table are fundamentally different. Equation B derives the blur circle, whereas A describes a separability criterion. If both were in agreement, A should be the radius of the blur circle B, in arithmetic terms: $\delta_t = CoC_{obj}$. In Gojani's final formulation of $\delta_t$, they drop one of their terms, which makes $\delta_t$ agree with Schwarz. However, this work proves that their reasoning to drop the term containing $\Delta_t$ is weak as both terms in their formulation amount to the same value; the proof can be found in Appendix \ref{Appendix: Blurred Object}.

\par To better understand the difference between the two formulations and how they relate, it is worth explaining the reasoning behind them. In their paper, Gojani et al. make the fundamental assumption that anything inside the $CoC$ is indistinguishable. Therefore, the smallest distinguishable size is obtained by tracing the cone of light from the $CoC$ in the sensor plane to a size in the out-of-focus object plane. Figure \ref{fig: Gojani cone of light} shows the cone of light used by Gojani et al. in their derivation. On the image it is possible to see distances that can be translated to the convention presented in Figure \ref{fig: BOS setup Raffel}, $s_t$ corresponds to the defocus distance $l$, $s_o$ is the in-focus distance $d_f$ and $s_i$ is the distance between the lens and the sensor $Z$. Equation B is already a translation from Equation 12 of Gojani's paper to this thesis' nomenclature.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/Gojani cone of light diagram.jpg}
    \caption[Cone of light reaching the sensor]{Diagram of the cone of light that reaches a length $\Delta_t$ in the sensor. This image was used by Gojani et al. to derive their equation for spatial resolution. Credit to Gojani et al. \cite{Gojani&Obayashi}}
    \label{fig: Gojani cone of light}
\end{figure}

\par In Gojani's derivation of the blur circle $\delta_t$, the length in the sensor plane $\Delta_t$ is explicitly called the ``circle of confusion ($CoC$)'' \cite{Gojani&Obayashi}. Ambiguity arises when they subsequently define it as a sampling cell $\Delta_t = k \cdot l_{px}$, where $k$ is a small integer and $l_{px}$ is the pixel pitch. It is not explained how $k$ is computed and $\Delta_t$ is fixed by them in the range of 5--50 \si{\micro\metre}. Appendix \ref{Appendix: Blurred Object} demonstrates that, if Greenleaf's equation for the $CoC$ \cite{Greenleaf1950} is used to compute $\Delta_t$, it yields a blur circle two times bigger than $CoC_{obj}$, that is, $\delta_t = 2 \cdot CoC_{obj}$, contradicting Schwarz's formulation. The established range for $\Delta_t$ was also found to be violated for certain cases. Furthermore, no systematic experimental validation corroborating Gojani's claims has been found in the literature. The closest found was its use by Michalski \cite{Michalski2018}, in an attempt to compare it with the maximum density gradient width of a jet. Still, Michalski found it to be only in a comparable order of magnitude to the computed value (width of 1.66 mm and $\delta_t$ of 0.86 mm).

\par In contrast to approach B, Schwarz and Braukmann correlated $CoC$ with spatial resolution experimentally \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann}. First, they transferred the $CoC$ to the refractive object plane by using a blurred object magnification, thus defining a $CoC_{obj}$ (this transfer was also derived in Appendix \ref{Appendix: Blurred Object}). Then, they obtained the smallest distinguishable size by measuring the contrast in light intensity of bright and dark bars of a resolution target for various optical setups. Schwarz's figure for spatial resolution is thus based on an established separability criterion, which defines how close two points may lie before their image is merged. It was found that the minimum discernible feature was around half the $CoC_{obj}$, $s_{min} \approx 0.5 \cdot CoC_{obj}$.

\par After an analysis of the two formulations, it can ultimately be said that the experimental validation makes Schwarz's formulation the stronger of the two. Additionally, the loose definition of $\Delta_t$ in Gojani's formulation leads to ambiguity that can be interpreted into a result two times larger than the one corresponding to Schwarz's measurements. Therefore, Schwarz's formulation should be used to estimate the optical spatial resolution of a BOS setup. This is the one used in this work and is presented in Equation \ref{equation: smin}.

\par Up to this point, spatial resolution and sensitivity have been described primarily as functions of the optical setup. However, these performance parameters are also significantly influenced by the choice of post-processing algorithms and hardware. Different image processing techniques can achieve varying levels of sensitivity and spatial resolution when converting captured images into displacement fields. The background pattern parameters, such as speckle size and frequency, also limit spatial resolution and influence the choice of post-processing algorithms. Consequently, each of these aspects must be carefully selected for an experiment \cite{Schmidt2025}.

\subsection{Imaging Hardware and Post-Processing}
\label{subsection: Image processing}

\par On the imaging hardware level, the main parameter that influences resolution is the pixel density, which quantifies how many pixels correspond to a millimeter in the image. It is related to the magnification but also the size of the sensor and its pixels \cite{Schmidt2025, Gojani&Obayashi}. A smaller pixel size and higher magnification provide a higher pixel density, which means that a smaller feature can be imaged with more pixels, providing more detail. It is worth noting that there is no use in having a higher spatial distribution of pixels if the blur limits the size of the smallest distinguishable feature. Consequently, an optimization must take place where the optical spatial resolution matches the hardware and post-processing resolution, so that the setup resources are used efficiently. Schwarz and Braukmann make precisely this comparison by expressing both the circle of confusion and the interrogation window in the object domain and evaluating each against the size of the structure under study \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann}.

\par Once the BOS images are taken, they must be processed in a step that converts the recorded images into displacement field data. The algorithm chosen for it limits both the accuracy and the spatial resolution of the result. Cross-correlation (CC) was the first method applied to the technique \cite{Raffel2001} and remains the most widely used. Coming directly from its use in PIV, it divides the reference and the deflected image into interrogation windows, and uses a cross-correlation function applied to the intensity distribution of each window pair to find the displacement that best aligns them \cite{Schmidt2025}. One vector is returned per window, so the spacing of the resulting field follows from the interrogation window size $IW$ and the fractional overlap $o$ as $IW(1-o)$. For example, a 64 pixel window at $75\%$ overlap yields a vector every 16 pixels.

\par The inheritance from PIV comes with its drawbacks. Schmidt et al. \cite{Schmidt2025} point out two differences between the applications. Displacements in BOS are of the order of a few pixels rather than the ten to twenty typical of PIV \cite{Schmidt2025, Raffel2015}, so the dynamic range that CC is designed to deliver goes largely unused while its penalty in accuracy and resolution is still present. Raffel notes that although displacements below half a pixel can still be determined with high accuracy, the smaller signal leaves BOS with relative errors of the order of 2--3\% of full scale against roughly 1\% for PIV \cite{Raffel2015}. Furthermore, the background is manufactured in BOS as a field of discrete bright dots imitating tracer particles, which is not the best suited pattern to resolve a smooth displacement field \cite{Schmidt2025, CakirEtAl}.

\par These shortcomings motivated several alternatives. Iterative least squares (ILS) uses CC to locate the integer-pixel displacement and computes the subpixel part through a separate fit. This improves subpixel accuracy and reduces sensitivity to imaging noise. Optical flow (OF) abandons windows altogether and solves for the whole field at once, returning a vector at every pixel. However, it comes with a computational cost roughly an order of magnitude above CC. Fourier demodulation (FCD) recovers the deflections from phase shifts in the Fourier domain and is faster than both, but demands a periodic background such as a sinusoid or a checkerboard. Figure \ref{fig: BOS processing algorithms} displays a comparison of the performance of these different methods on idealized synthetic images \cite{Schmidt2025}. It shows that OF and FCD reproduced the ground truth almost exactly, while CC and ILS visibly undersampled sharp gradients. Cakir et al. \cite{CakirEtAl} also reported a clear advantage of OF over CC in both accuracy and the range of resolvable density gradient amplitudes on a Prandtl-Meyer expansion fan.

\par Those margins narrow considerably in practice. Schmidt et al. \cite{Schmidt2025} observe that the advantage of a higher-fidelity algorithm is realized mainly when small-scale structures dominate and the images are essentially free of noise, and that defocusing can render them obsolete by blurring the fine scales out before processing begins. Schwarz and Braukmann \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann} reach the same conclusion independently, reporting that in their own evaluations with synthetic BOS images they could not, using optical flow, reliably match the resolution and accuracy of a $12 \times 12$ pixel cross-correlation. Since a BOS setup is deliberately defocused on the refractive object, this blurring applies directly, which means that the advantage of a higher-fidelity algorithm could just go unused. Moreover, considering that a laser-speckled pattern cannot provide the periodic background FCD demands, and that OF carries an order-of-magnitude computational cost, the practical balance favors cross-correlation with an optional iterative least squares refinement.

\par Within CC, the interrogation window governs the compromise between robustness and resolution. Indeed, Schwarz and Braukmann name the window size and the geometric blur as the two factors that jointly limit the spatial resolution of a BOS measurement \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann}, so the optical and processing constraints cannot be chosen independently. Small windows are preferred in BOS precisely because the displacements are small \cite{Schmidt2025}, yet a window holding too few features yields an unreliable correlation peak. The minimum window size recommended in the literature ranges from four or five to ten background dots, depending on the author \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann}. Kähler et al. \cite{KahlerEtAl} quantified the resulting limit through the step response width, the smallest separation at which two vectors are genuinely independent, and showed it to grow both with the window size and the diameter of the imaged features. This means that an increase in the overlap produces a denser vector field without improving that independence, so the final window size, and not the vector spacing, is what sets the true spatial resolution of a measurement.

\par The same analysis determines the size of the background dots. Reliable subpixel interpolation of the correlation peak requires dots larger than roughly two pixels. Below that threshold the fit can no longer locate the peak and the estimated displacements tend toward integer values, an effect known as peak locking \cite{KahlerEtAl}. Large dots are equally unhelpful, since the minimum distance between independent vectors grows with the dot diameter. Once the dots reach about a quarter of the window size, the ones cut by the window edges degrade the signal response sharply. Holding the size of the background dots between roughly two and five pixels satisfies both limitations. BOS literature reaches the same interval experimentally: Raffel \cite{Raffel2015} reports background structures typically imaged at three to five pixels, with lower values rarely attainable because of diffraction at the small apertures BOS demands. He also cautions that the three-point Gaussian peak fit inherited from PIV adds noise once the dots grow beyond that range. Schwarz and Braukmann quote an optimum dot image of about three pixels and worked at 3.4 to 5 pixels in their own experiments \cite{Schwarz&Braukmann}. For speckle backgrounds the same target applies: Michalski et al. sized their speckles at 61.6 \si{\micro\metre} to cover roughly four pixels \cite{Michalski2018}. This requirement is respected by the optical design of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} and investigated by the Speckle Pattern Experiment, Sections \ref{section: Speckle Pattern Investigation} and \ref{section: Speckle Pattern Experiment Results}.

\par Processing and optics therefore impose complementary limits on what a BOS measurement can resolve. Neither has confined the technique to a single form: over the past twenty-five years it has been adapted to three-dimensional fields, to scales ranging from millimeters to aircraft in flight, and to backgrounds of very different kinds. Those developments are reviewed next.

\subsection{Variants of BOS}
\label{subsection: Variants of BOS}
\par Since BOS was introduced in 2000, the technique has not remained static; various authors have worked on modifications that enabled its application where it would be impractical to do so with the standard method. These developments were surveyed by Raffel \cite{Raffel2015} at the fifteen-year mark and, more recently, by Schmidt et al. \cite{Schmidt2025} across the full twenty-five years. A significant evolution was the development of tomographic BOS, which combines multiple camera perspectives to reconstruct three-dimensional density fields \cite{TomographicBOS, Raffel2015}. This method was demonstrated in applications ranging from supersonic wind tunnel tests to combustion imaging, and has since become the principal route to unsteady structures in variable-density flows \cite{Schmidt2025}. For setups where multiple cameras are unavailable, plenoptic BOS offers an alternative by capturing various perspectives from a single camera through a micro-lens array, enabling independent refocusing at different planes along the line of sight from a single image \cite{PlenopticBOS}. These modifications have greatly expanded the scope of BOS applications beyond single 2D gradient imaging.

\par Other studies have focused on the limits of scaling BOS. At small scales, telecentric BOS addresses the geometric blur inherent to conventional setups through collection of parallel light rays onto the sensor \cite{TelecentricBOS}. This way perspective distortion is reduced and spatial resolution improved for fine-scale measurements. Van Hinsberg and Rösgen \cite{vanHinsberg2014} developed what they called Near-Field BOS. They modified the correction factor in the BOS relation presented by Dalziel et al. \cite{Dalziel2000} so that reliable densities can still be recovered when the background sits closely behind the flow. This removes the usual requirement for large background-to-object distances. At larger scales, airborne BOS (AirBOS) imaged supersonic aircraft in flight using only Earth's natural surface texture as a background \cite{AirBOS, AirBOS2}. Figure \ref{fig: AirBOS} shows the shock structure of two T-38 aircraft in formation captured with AirBOS. Another method (BOSCO) has used celestial objects such as the Sun as background \cite{BOSCO}. Outdoor explosive and ballistic tests have also leveraged natural backgrounds and zebra board patterns to visualize shock wave propagation over fields of view inaccessible to laboratory optics \cite{ExplosionsBOS, Schmidt2025}. These applications show that modified BOS can be applied in contexts where normal schlieren would have been impractical because it would need unreasonably heavy and large optical elements.

Both frames show the same measurement with a different color map. Credit to NASA, from Heineck et al. \cite{AirBOS2}}
    \label{fig: AirBOS}

\par For high-speed applications that demand high luminous intensity, experimentalists have developed different background illumination strategies. Back-illuminated transparent patterns, screen displayed background patterns and retroreflective coated surfaces have been demonstrated as effective solutions in test facilities \cite{RBOS, BackIluminatedBOS, Kurilov2026}. These methods try to overcome the constraints imposed by the small apertures needed by BOS to limit blur. The small lens entrance also starves the sensor of light, requiring more illumination. More luminous intensity also allows the use of shorter exposure times, which reduce motion blur. The same design pressure has driven sensor technology such as pulsed and high-framing-rate BOS \cite{Raffel2015}. In that context, event-based cameras record only pixel-level brightness changes, which allows them to reach kHz rates at a fraction of the illumination power and cost \cite{Schmidt2025}. More recently, laser speckle has been proposed as a background source due to its high intensity and the capability to access unusual defocus configurations as the background is always in focus \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. The state of the art of this last method will be further investigated next.

\section{Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren}
\label{section: Laser Speckled BOS}
\par Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren is based on the same principles as BOS, with the distinct difference that a laser speckled pattern is used as reference instead of a physical background pattern. The speckle pattern is a result of the constructive and destructive interference caused by the interaction of scattered wavefronts originating from a previously collimated coherent light source, which arrive at the sensor with random phase differences. This interference pattern has been extensively investigated since the 1960s \cite{NikitaFominBook, Goodman2020} and has found applications in diversified fields such as the imaging of blood flow in medical fields \cite{Boas&Dunn2010}, non-destructive analysis of structural materials through shearography \cite{Hung&Ho2005} and the study of density gradients using laser speckle photography \cite{Debrus1972}. However, the first reported use of a laser speckle pattern as a BOS background was by Meier and Rösgen in 2013 \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. It can be said then that SBOS is a relatively new development and, being this recent, it is yet to be widely applied and studied, presenting the opportunity for novel and interesting developments.

\par Speckle creation has been extensively treated by Goodman \cite{Goodman2020} and its application to fluid mechanics measurements by Fomin \cite{NikitaFominBook}. As aforementioned, the speckles are created when light gets scattered by a surface rougher than its wavelength, which randomly changes the amplitude and the optical path length of the scattered rays and thus creates an interference pattern. This surface can be a rough projection plane or a diffuser such as a ground glass. The speckles can either be objective, when the scattered light propagates freely to an observation plane; or subjective, when they are collected by a lens. It is worth highlighting that speckle creation is random at its core \cite{Cloud2007, Goodman2020}. As a consequence, the theoretical mathematical treatment of this phenomenon involves complex Fourier analysis and statistics, which were considered out of scope. This thesis will focus on the practical aspects of speckle generation and try to develop practical guidelines for its use.

\par The work of Meier and Rösgen \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013} introduced SBOS and showed that using a laser speckled background greatly expands the design space of a BOS experimental setup. By creating subjective speckles, the exit pupil of the imaging system itself can be regarded as the effective scattering spot \cite{Goodman2020}. Consequently, the speckles stay sharp at any virtual position the camera has its focus on. This permits the positioning of the pattern to be virtually anywhere along the laser beam, be it in front of the refractive object, inside of it, behind it, or even beyond the scattering plane. Consequently, optical arrangements that would be impossible in conventional BOS become feasible with SBOS. A picture of their experimental setup can be seen in Figure \ref{fig: SBOS Meier and Roesgen}.

\par These new optical configurations promise great benefits compared to standard BOS \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. By focusing the camera in front of the test section, a larger increase in sensitivity can be achieved while maintaining the same spatial resolution. Positioning the focus beyond the physical position of the screen allows for larger effective defocusing distances while maintaining the screen close to the test section, which greatly reduces the physical size of the setup. Focusing the camera inside the test section promises to improve the spatial resolution; however, the implications for image quality and sensitivity are not fully clear. Meier and Rösgen also developed the double-pass mode, where the laser beam is directed coaxially with the camera through the test section, effectively doubling the sensitivity by capturing the deflection of both the illumination beam and the imaging path. The ability to choose among these optical configurations alleviates the constraints between sensitivity and spatial resolution present in standard BOS, allowing for a much more optimized optical setup.

\par Furthermore, replacing the conventional printed background with a laser-generated speckle pattern offers advantages in terms of background quality and its spatial frequency content. Unlike physical background patterns, whose feature size is constrained by printing and manufacturing limitations, the minimum speckle diameter is only limited by the Rayleigh diffraction criterion \cite{Barker&Fourney}. If the interference pattern becomes diffraction limited due to the camera aperture, it will be constrained only by the laser wavelength, the f-stop number and the camera magnification. Consequently, SBOS can produce significantly finer background structures, providing higher spatial-frequency information for image correlation and potentially improving the spatial resolution.

\par After the work of Meier and Rösgen, only a limited number of studies have explored SBOS experimentally. Bühlmann \cite{Buhlmann2020} employed the technique in the tomographic imaging of a heated jet near a wall where the laser-speckle was projected as a background. His measurements highlight the potential of SBOS as they would have been unfeasible with conventional BOS, given the proximity of the background to a hot jet. Michalski et al. \cite{Michalski2018} applied the method using the in-focus plane in front of the refractive object to obtain the calorimetry of an electrical spark discharge, thus highlighting the usefulness of SBOS for imaging fast phenomena with low signal-to-noise ratio. In contrast to the previously mentioned works that used a scattering screen, Michalski et al. used a ground glass diffuser to scatter the laser beam and pointed it directly to the camera sensor, much like the setup used by Debrus on speckle photography \cite{Debrus1972}, shown in Figure \ref{fig: SBOS setup illustrated}. Moreover, their work rigorously quantified the method's uncertainties and compared its accuracy with standard BOS, which will be discussed in the next section. Nakamura et al. \cite{Nakamura2021} also employed laser speckles in a different way to develop a methodology intended to compute the depth location of refractive structures inside the test section and used a compressible jet to demonstrate it. These studies demonstrate the method's potential. Nevertheless, most of its developments have originated from a small number of research groups. Consequently, important questions regarding implementation, uncertainty, spatial resolution, and applicability in demanding environments remain insufficiently explored.

\subsection{Limitations, Accuracy and Uncertainty of SBOS}
\label{subsection: SBOS limitations}

\par Despite its advantages, SBOS presents some constraints and drawbacks that must be considered in its implementation. Some of these are shared with BOS such as spatial resolution and sensitivity constraints due to the chosen processing algorithms and hardware. Others are more characteristic to SBOS, such as higher complexity and possible decorrelation problems. This subsection will discuss how the literature treats these limitations and how it provides a comparison of the method's accuracies and uncertainties with standard BOS.

\par A natural complication of SBOS is the increased complexity that comes with using a laser instead of a physical background. It requires laser compatible lenses, more optical elements, special care when handling and maintaining the setup and additional safety concerns as the laser presents a hazard at high energy settings. By implementing the method, a trade-off must be made between the increased complication and the gains in resolution and sensitivity; this will be treated in more depth in the methodology chapter.

\par It is also worth noting that there have been two ways in the literature to generate the speckle pattern and both have been used successfully for SBOS. It is possible to classify them into projected speckles employed by Bühlmann \cite{Buhlmann2020} and Meier and Rösgen \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}, and directed speckles used by Michalski et al. \cite{Michalski2018} and Nakamura et al. \cite{Nakamura2021}. It is not clear which one of them is more effective in generating speckles, that is, what the drawbacks and advantages of each approach are. It might be only a matter of convenience when only one optical access is available, or one of the methods might present a more uniform distribution of speckles or be more prone to decorrelation than the other. Given the lack of literature on the topic, it would be interesting to test them both and compare them.

\par One substantial limitation encountered by Bühlmann on his projected laser speckle setup was decorrelation \cite{Buhlmann2020}. That is the name given to the failure to correlate the background shift with the reference. This phenomenon might arise when using a laser speckled background pattern because the speckles are interferometric by nature, meaning that perturbations in the diffuser surface do not move the pattern but change it altogether. Due to this issue, Bühlmann was required to use large cross-correlation windows and apply corrections for the pattern shift \cite{Buhlmann2020}. He identified the cause as the thermal expansion of the projection screen, since the heated jet ran along the opaque wall that served as the speckle background and deformed it, adding a pattern shift on top of the flow signal. This mechanism was modeled with relations adapted from electronic speckle photography and verified experimentally. The decorrelation proved considerably stronger for the jet running along the wall than for a free jet and it increased with the temperature of the jet \cite{Buhlmann2020}. That indicates that the diffuser surface should ideally be isolated from the flow to avoid decorrelation. This is corroborated by Li and Chiang who have researched decorrelation effects for laser speckle interferometry and concluded that the main sources of the effect are surface tilt, out-of-plane displacement and lens aberration \cite{Li&CHiang1986}. Meier and Rösgen have also mentioned linear induced errors due to a tilt of the projecting surface \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. Both Meier and Rösgen and Bühlmann \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013, Buhlmann2020} used projected speckles to generate their background patterns, so it might be that this speckle generation method is more exposed to decorrelation due to tilting of the surface, deformation by the flow or vibrations. Neither Nakamura et al. nor Michalski et al. have reported the issue in their direct laser-speckle setups, suggesting that the effect could be mitigated by this approach \cite{Nakamura2021, Michalski2018}.

\par Regarding the accuracy of the technique, both Michalski and Nakamura quantified errors in their applications \cite{Michalski2018, Nakamura2021}. Michalski et al. validated their implementation with a CO$_2$ jet and obtained a mean density for the core $0.8\% \pm 0.9\%$ lower than expected, yielding a $0.3\% \pm 0.3\%$ error for the final measured CO$_2$ density. Nakamura et al. described an approximated error of $10\%$ on their method to identify the depth position of refractive objects, but did not show the errors of their density field. Compared to BOS, whose measurement error has been found to be on the order of 2--3\% of the full scale by Raffel \cite{Raffel2015} and Elsinga et al. \cite{Elsinga2004} reporting an accuracy of around $2\%$ against theory for a Prandtl--Meyer expansion fan, SBOS applied by Michalski et al. performs well in terms of accuracy.

\par Uncertainty and noise are also important aspects to ascertain in experimental techniques. Michalski et al. \cite{Michalski2018} and Meier and Rösgen \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013} mention that the increased sensitivity of SBOS also makes it more prone to noise from vibration coming from setup equipment. Moreover, noise could come intrinsically from the camera sensor, room temperature gradients and even the image processing algorithm. Michalski et al. estimated the total uncertainty in their setup, quantifying partial uncertainties from the integration method, magnification, defocusing distance, cross-correlation, tomographic reconstruction and white noise, reaching a final value of $6.2\%$. In this project, the uncertainty sources of the designed setup will be documented and their impact quantified in Chapter \ref{chapter: Results}.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{section: Research Gap}

\par The literature review reveals that SBOS is a promising but still infant technique. While Meier and Rösgen established its conceptual foundation and demonstrated some of its advantages over standard BOS \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}, the subsequent body of work remains sparse, originating largely from a small number of research groups and covering a narrow range of applications. This contrasts sharply with the extensive and well-validated literature surrounding conventional BOS. Three clusters of open questions emerge from this review. First, no systematic implementation guidelines exist for SBOS setup design. The sensitivity-resolution trade-off is well characterized for BOS, but whether and how those relationships translate to SBOS configurations, particularly for unusual background placements, has not been thoroughly addressed. Second, there is no comprehensive experimental report on the effects of positioning the reference background inside the test section. Indeed, Meier and Rösgen \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013} presented what was called speckle interferometer double pass SBOS, which had the refractive object in-focus. However, no instance in the literature has been found of a single pass method that placed the reference inside the test section. Third, the behavior of SBOS in demanding high-speed wind tunnel environments is entirely unexplored, leaving open questions about decorrelation, vibration sensitivity, and optical feasibility under those conditions.

\par Together, these gaps mean that a researcher wishing to implement SBOS today would find no coherent roadmap to follow. The present thesis addresses this by designing, building, and systematically investigating an SBOS setup, using a high-speed wind tunnel application as the target demonstration case. The specific research questions motivating this work are stated in Chapter \ref{chapter: introduction}.
