\chapter{From Compressibility to Light Refraction}
\label{chapter: Theory}

\par A brief theoretical background is needed to understand how the relationship between compressibility and refraction culminates in optical flow visualization techniques. The next sections will first explain how high speeds give rise to density changes in a fluid. It will highlight the importance of the Mach Number, give a description of compression and expansion waves, and show how they can be applied in real cases such as compressible jets and aerospike nozzles. Afterwards it will treat refraction, stressing the most important aspects that make Schlieren imaging possible. This knowledge will be important to understanding the scientific principles that make SBOS possible and interpret the results of the experimental campaigns realized throughout this thesis.


\section{Compressibility and Density}
\label{section: Compressibility}

\par Optical density measurement techniques were envisioned to study the variability of density in a fluid. The property of a fluid to change its density is called compressibility. Air is a gas, so its molecules are distanced enough that they are able to move closer together when some pressure is applied. Due to this, air is classified as having high compressibility. When objects move slowly through it, changes in pressure throughout the flow field are small enough that the effect can be neglected as an engineering approximation, and density can be assumed constant. This is the incompressible assumption, $d\rho \approx 0$. The variation of density $\rho$ with pressure $p$ is described by the compressibility coefficient $\tau$ (at constant entropy or temperature),through the equation: $d \rho =\rho \tau dp$.

\par When high speeds are achieved, the pressure gradients through the flow field become larger, leading to a significant change in density that needs to be accounted for. A normal threshold for compressible flows is when the Mach Number ($M$) surpasses 0.3, which marks the point where density changes are higher than $5\%$ \cite{TheBookOfAnderson}. The Mach number is a non-dimensional quantity, defined in Equation \ref{equation:Mach Number} as the ratio between the flow velocity $V$ and the speed of sound $a$. It governs the behavior of waves traveling faster than the speed of sound, called compression waves. These waves were first described by Riemann and experimentally observed in 1885 by Ernst Mach and Wentzel with schlieren imaging \cite{Settles2001}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation:Mach Number}
    M = \frac{V}{a}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/Moving sound waves.png}
    \caption[Information propagation at subsonic and supersonic speeds]{Propagation of information by an object moving at (a) subsonic speeds and (b) supersonic speeds, credit to Anderson \cite{TheBookOfAnderson}}
    \label{fig: Moving sound wave}
\end{figure}

\par compression waves form when an object moves faster than the sound waves emitted by it. This makes the sound waves pile up and coalesce, forming a standing wave in front of the body \cite{TheBookOfAnderson}. Figure \ref{fig: Moving sound wave} shows an object leaving point A at a speed faster than the speed of sound. After a time interval $t$ it has traveled $Vt$, while the first emitted wave has only propagated a distance $at$. A compression wave is created tangentially to the emitted sound waves and has an angle $\mu$ called the Mach Angle. The angle is derived geometrically from Figure \ref{fig: Moving sound wave} and is inversely proportional to $M$, as described in Equation \ref{equation: Mach Angle}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Mach Angle}
    \sin \mu = \frac{a t}{V t} = \frac{1}{M}
\quad \Leftrightarrow \quad
\mu = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)
\end{equation}

\par The coalescence of the emitted waves creates an extremely thin region (of the order of $10^{-7}$ m) where the flow properties change drastically, in an almost explosive compression process that produces a seemingly discontinuous pressure increase across it, called a shock \cite{TheBookOfAnderson}. When the emitted sound from the object becomes stronger, an oblique shock wave with an angle $\beta$ from the freestream air is formed, where $\beta > \mu$. Figure \ref{fig: Oblique shock geometry} permits the derivation of the relationship between the thermodynamic properties of the fluid before and after an oblique shock. Studying the control volume $abcdef$ the continuity defines Equation \ref{equation: oblique continuity}, momentum is described in Equation \ref{equation: oblique momentum} and energy is given by Equation \ref{equation: oblique energy}. This equations allow to predict that through an oblique shock: the pressure, density, temperature and entropy of the flow increase, while the total pressure, Mach number and velocity decrease. the change in density across a shock is what allows it to be visualized by schlieren techniques.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/Oblique shock geometry.png}
    \caption[Oblique shock geometry]{Oblique shock geometry, credit to Anderson \cite{TheBookOfAnderson}}
    \label{fig: Oblique shock geometry}
\end{figure}

\begin{align}
\rho_1u_1 &= \rho_2u_2 \label{equation: oblique continuity} \\
p_1 + \rho_1 u_1^2 &= p_2 + \rho_2 u_2^2 \label{equation: oblique momentum} \\
h_1 + \frac{u_1^2}{2} &= h_2 + \frac{u_2^2}{2} \label{equation: oblique energy}
\end{align}

\par It is worth noting that the thermodynamic properties described by Equations \ref{equation: oblique continuity}, \ref{equation: oblique momentum} and \ref{equation: oblique energy} depend only on the velocity component normal to the shock. Considering a calorically perfect gas and adiabatic flow across the shock, it is possible to obtain another set of relationships that relate the ratio of the properties after and before the shock, with only the normal Mach number before the shock $M_{n,1}$ and the gas specific heat ratio $\gamma$. Equations \ref{equation: Mach n1}, \ref{equation: oblique density ratio} and \ref{equation: oblique pressure ratio} describe how the difference in pressure and density across a shock tends to increase with a higher $M_1$ and $\beta$. These are important equations, as they allow the computation of the conditions after the shock by only knowing the initial flow condition and the shock angle.

\begin{align}
M_{n,1} &= M_1 \sin\beta, \label{equation: Mach n1}\\
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}
&=
\frac{(\gamma + 1)M_{n,1}^{2}}
{2 + (\gamma - 1)M_{n,1}^{2}}, \label{equation: oblique density ratio} \\
\frac{p_2}{p_1}
&=
1 + \left(\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma + 1}\right)
\left(M_{n,1}^{2} - 1\right). \label{equation: oblique pressure ratio}
\end{align}

Another important relationship defines the flow direction $\theta$ after it has passed through the oblique shock, called the $\theta-\beta-M$ relation. For a given $M_1$ and $\theta$ in flows over concave wedges, this equation gives two solutions for $\beta$, defining a weak and a strong shock. The classification of a shock as strong or weak relates to the total pressure loss across it: the bigger the total pressure loss, the stronger the shock. Consequently, the bigger the density gradient as well. The relationship described in Equation \ref{equation: oblique density ratio} shows how strong density gradients naturally appear in supersonic flow across shock waves. Therefore, If the density gradients through a fluid is know, a shock can be identified by a small region with a sharp gradient pointing in the flow direction, or slightly oblique to it.

\begin{equation}
\label{equation:theta-beta-M}
    \tan \theta = 2 \cot \beta \frac{M_1^2\sin^2\beta -1}{M_1^2(\gamma +\cos 2\beta )+2}
\end{equation}

A supersonic flow can not only compress through compressible waves or shocks but also expand through a Prandtl-Meyer expansion fan. When a supersonic flow finds a convex corner instead of a concave one, it turns away from itself, creating a continuous low pressure zone that accelerates it even further. An important parameter in these scenarios is the Prandtl-Meyer Angle $\nu$, which can be used to know how much the flow must bend in order to achieve a certain Mach number $M_2$. The Prandtl-Meyer angle described in Equation \ref{equation: Prandtl-Meyer function} can be used to describe the subsequent flow direction through Equation \ref{equation: Prandtl-Meyer turning angle}. If the flow direction after the expansion fan is known, these equations can be used to find the Mach number after the fan, which in turn can be used to find the pressure and density because the flow through a fan is Isentropic.

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: Prandtl-Meyer function}
    \nu(M) = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1}} \tan^{-1} \sqrt{\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma + 1}\left(M^2 - 1\right)} - \tan^{-1} \sqrt{M^2 - 1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: Prandtl-Meyer turning angle}
    \theta = \nu(M_2) - \nu(M_1)
\end{equation}

\par An isentropic process means that entropy is conserved through it. No change in entropy signals that no losses are present, consequently total quantities such as temperature and pressure are also maintained. Through the isentropic relationships in Equations \ref{equation: isentropic pressure} and \ref{equation: isentropic density}, the static density and pressure after an expansion fan can be calculated. These equations describe how an increase in the Mach number leads to a decrease in both static density and pressure. Thus, in a technique where density gradients are visualized expansion fans can be identified through a continuous region where the gradient is opposite to the flow direction.

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: isentropic pressure}
    \frac{p_0}{p} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: isentropic density}
    \frac{\rho_0}{\rho} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma - 1}}
\end{equation}


\par This section clarifies the connection between compressible flow and density changes, by explaining how high speeds generate compression and expansion waves that vary the flow's properties. The theory presented here can be used to interpret two notable study cases, the flow of a free high-speed jet and the flow structure of an aerospike nozzle exhaust, which will be used later as applications for the SBOS technique. Since these variations in density produce corresponding variations in refractive index, shock and expansion structures can be detected using the deflection of light passing through the medium by employing schlieren methods. These techniques allow the visualization and quantification of the resulting density gradients, enabling experimental measurements to be compared with theoretical predictions.

\section{Compressible Jets}
\label{section: Compressible Jet Theory}

One important case study in compressible aerodynamics is the flow structure of a high-pressure jet. A jet forms when a high-pressure flow passes through a nozzle and exits into a lower-pressure environment. The flow through the nozzle influences the exiting jet. Nozzle flow is governed by the ratio of the total pressure at the nozzle inlet to the ambient pressure, the nozzle pressure ratio $NPR = p_0/p_a$. When the $NPR$ exceeds a critical value, $NPR_c$, the Mach number at the nozzle's minimum cross-section, the throat, reaches its maximum at 1 and the flow is said to be choked. For a certain throat diameter and temperature, the mass flow rate is no longer dependent on the ambient pressure and grows proportionally to $p_0$. If the nozzle diverges downstream of the throat, the supersonic flow expands, the static pressure falls, and the flow accelerates to higher Mach numbers. The degree of expansion, set by the exit-to-throat area ratio, determines the static pressure at the nozzle exit, $p_e$.

The relation between $p_e$ and the ambient pressure $p_a$ classifies the jet. It is under-expanded if $p_e > p_a$, perfectly expanded if $p_e = p_a$, and over-expanded if $p_e < p_a$, and each regime has its own flow features. As sketched in Figure \ref{fig: jet expansion regimes}, in an under-expanded jet the shear layer bends away from the centerline, in an over-expanded jet it is pinched toward the centerline, and a perfectly expanded jet remains aligned with the nozzle axis \cite{Hagemann1998}. One of the experimental campaigns of this work will use jets generated by an air gun with a simple convergent duct operated above $NPR_c$. In this convergent nozzle the throat coincides with the exit, so the flow is choked at the exit with $p_e > p_a$, making the jet under-expanded. Therefore, this section will focus on the flow structure of under-expanded jets. The theory presented here will help interpret the density gradient fields that will be obtained through SBOS.

\end{figure}

\par Figure \ref{fig: planar underexpanded jet} shows the analytical solution of a planar under-expanded jet. It shows that the jet is marked by periodic structures called shock cells. These cells form as a mechanism of pressure adaptation between the nozzle exit and the ambient air through alternating Prandtl-Meyer expansion and compression waves \cite{vanHinsberg2014}. Since the pressure at the nozzle exit is greater than the ambient pressure, the flow needs to expand to match the outside pressure. The result is a Prandtl-Meyer expansion fan anchored at the lip of the nozzle. These expansion waves are reflected at the axis of symmetry into another expansion fan, which in turn reflects at the jet boundary into compression waves. These converge into one another and coalesce, forming a shock. This shock increases the pressure, leading to another round of expansion waves. This process continues until, due to friction, the jet pressure equals the atmospheric pressure.

\end{figure}

\par The three Figures \ref{fig: moderately underexpanded jet}, \ref{fig: highly underexpanded jet} and \ref{fig: unique barrel underexpanded jet} show sketches of under-expanded circular jets at different $NPR$s \cite{Duronio2023}. Due to its axisymmetry, the circular jet has a different structure from the planar case, displaying an early formation of an oblique shock extending from the lip to the jet axis, often called \emph{Intercepting shock}. When the $NPR$ is between 2 and 4, as in Figure \ref{fig: moderately underexpanded jet}, this oblique shock reflects at the jet axis and goes to the shear layer, where it turns into an expansion fan, thus forming an ``X'' shape at each shock cell. For higher $NPR$s between 4 and 7, Figure \ref{fig: highly underexpanded jet}, the intercepting shock transitions to a singular reflection and produces a normal shock called \emph{Mach disk}, which gives each shock cell a ``barrel'' shape. For even higher $NPR$s, Figure \ref{fig: unique barrel underexpanded jet}, the jet is dominated by a single barrel. The last case is marked by a decreased jet boundary diameter and a very long plume caused by entraining air.

\par As hinted by the scale of the sketches in Figure \ref{fig: circular underexpanded jets}, the $NPR$ also changes the length of the shock cells, $L_s$. Robert Emden was the first to correlate a weighted average of the $L_s$ of under-expanded choked jets with the $NPR$ in 1899 \cite{Powell2010, Emden1899}. After a series of experiments he reached the format of Equation \ref{equation: Emden Formula}, where $d_N$ is the nozzle exit diameter and $k$ is a numerical coefficient that varies with the nozzle geometry and $NPR$ range. The value of $k$ changed from 0.77 to 1.02 during the campaign, so Emden averaged the results of different nozzles to obtain $k = 0.88$. Later, Hartmann and Lazarus \cite{Hartmann1941} used Emden's format with the average of all the cells except the first, achieving $k = 1.06$. They also correlated only the first shock cell length and introduced a constant term independent of $NPR$, obtaining the format of Equation \ref{equation: Lazarus-Hartmann Formula}, with $k = 0.98$ and $b = 0.3$.
\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Emden Formula}
    \frac{L_s}{d_N} = k\sqrt{NPR - NPR_c}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Lazarus-Hartmann Formula}
    \frac{L_s}{d_N} = k\sqrt{NPR - NPR_c}+b
\end{equation}

\section{Aerospike Nozzle Flow}
\label{section: aerospike nozzle theory}
\par The final application of SBOS in this thesis will be the implementation of SBOS to image the flow through an aerospike nozzle. The distinct feature of an aerospike nozzle is the presence of its ramp right after its exhaust. The ramp is designed so that the expansion fan anchored at the outside lip is not disturbed by the reflection of its own waves \cite{Wisse2005}. In the design condition, the flow expands through these waves, accelerating and reducing its static pressure, until design conditions are met. In the over-expanded condition, a similar pressure adaptation process that happened in the free under-expanded jet now occurs in the ramp, where the flow is redirected to be parallel to the centerline \cite{Hagemann1998}. The result is a passive pressure adaptation mechanism that allows an aerospike to keep its thrust efficiency for a wide range of ambient pressures. In under-expanded condition, the NPR of the aerospike nozzle is higher than design so the flow is not able to fully expand when it reaches the centerline. The ambient pressure is only reached downstream of the nozzle, bending the expansion fans away from the centerline and increasing the maximum turning angle of the fan. This redirects part of the exhaust flow sideways which leads to a loss in thrust efficiency. Consequently, engineers try to avoid this regime in favor of over-expanded and perfectly expanded flow, by designing the aerospike for near vacuum conditions. Figure \ref{fig: aerospike expansion regimes} shows the flow structure of an aerospike in under-expanded, perfectly expanded and over-expanded conditions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/under perfect and over expanded aerospike.jpg}
    \caption[Linear aerospike flow structure across expansion regimes]{Flow structure of a linear aerospike nozzle in under-expanded, perfectly expanded and over-expanded conditions, modified from Wisse \cite{Wisse2005}}
    \label{fig: aerospike expansion regimes}
\end{figure}

\par It is the pressure adaptation feature present in an aerospike at over-expanded condition that makes it interesting for Single Stage to Orbit (SSTO) vehicles \cite{Hagemann1998}. As shown in Figure \ref{fig: aerospike expansion regimes}, the process resembles the succession of expansion and compression waves present in the free under-expanded jet of Figure \ref{fig: planar underexpanded jet}. The initial waves reach the ramp and due to its concave geometry, the flow is compressed and reflects as compression waves. In the shear layer, the compression waves reflect into waves of the other type and are redirected to the ramp. At this stage, however, the curvature of the ramp is less accentuated and the waves reflect at the ramp into ones of the same family. The process continues with waves changing their type at the jet boundary and maintaining it when hitting the ramp, gradually fluctuating the ramp pressure around ambient conditions. This way, the flow is directed to be parallel to the centerline and there are no abrupt pressure differences capable of producing strong loss-inducing shocks.

\par The flow through the ramp of an aerospike nozzle will be investigated later using SBOS to demonstrate the imaging method capabilities. In that context, the description of the flow physics will be of utmost importance to assess whether the method has sufficient resolution and sensitivity to correctly study such a complex flow field. It is now clear from the previous sections how high speeds and geometry lead to different flow structures that vary the fluid's density. The next section will explain how density influences the bending of light moving through a fluid, which is the fundamental principle behind optical density measurement techniques.

\section{Refraction}
\label{section: Refraction}
\par Refraction is the change in direction of a light ray as it propagates through a medium with spatially varying refractive index. This index is the ratio between the speed of light through vacuum, $c_0 \approx 3 \cdot 10^8$ m/s, and the speed of light through the medium $c$, so that $n = c_0/c$.

\par The refractive index is a function of the light's wavelength $\lambda$ and the thermochemical state of the environment it passes through. If $n$ is known at a certain wavelength and thermochemical state, it can be extrapolated to other states through the Lorentz-Lorentz relation, described in Equation \ref{equation: Lorentz-Lorentz relation} where $A$ is the molar refractivity, dependent on the wavelength, and $W_M$ the mean molecular weight \cite{Schmidt2025}.

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: Lorentz-Lorentz relation}
    \frac{n^2 - 1}{\rho \left(n^2 + 2\right)}
=
\frac{A}{W_M}
=
\text{constant}
\end{equation}

\par The Gladstone-Dale relation described in Equation \ref{equation: Gladstone-Dale Relation} is the linearization of this relationship for gases, where $n$ is almost 1   and $K$ is the Gladstone-Dale coefficient \cite{Head2021, Settles2001}. This relationship shows that the refractive index is directly linked to the density of a gas.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Gladstone-Dale Relation}
    n = 1 + K \rho
\end{equation}

\par As shown in Figure \ref{fig: Diagrams of light refraction}, when light passes from one medium to another it can behave differently depending on the refractive index field of the subsequent medium. The light's interaction between two homogeneous media, shown in Figure \ref{fig: Diagrams of light refraction}a, is given by Snell's Law of, described in Equation \ref{equation: Snells law}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Snells law}
    n_1 \sin{\alpha_1} = n_2 \sin{\alpha_2}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/Diagrams of light refraction.png}
    \caption[Light refraction by Snell's Law and by an inhomogeneous medium]{Diagrams of Light refraction: (a) Change in the beam's direction between two homogeneous media as described by Snell's Law and (b) light refraction through an irregularly distributed refractive index environment. Adapted from Schmidt et al. \cite{Schmidt2025}, with the incidence and refraction angles relabeled $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$}
    \label{fig: Diagrams of light refraction}
\end{figure}

\par However, for inhomogeneous environments, as in Figure \ref{fig: Diagrams of light refraction}b, the change in light's direction is continuous. Consequently, a generalization must be performed to relate the deflection $\varepsilon$ to the refractive index field. Assuming an small angle deflection and small refractive index gradient Equation \ref{equation: continuous light deflection} is obtained, which describes how light how light continuously deflect in a medium, with $z$ being the line-of-sight direction \cite{Settles2001}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: continuous light deflection}
    d\varepsilon_y = \frac{1}{n_0}\frac{\partial n}{\partial y}\,dz
\end{equation}

\par The total deflection is the integral of $d\varepsilon$ along the ray path, as shown below. An analogous equation exists for $x$, by substituting $x$ for $y$.

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: displacement integral}
    \varepsilon_y
=
\frac{1}{n_0}
\int \frac{\partial n}{\partial y} dz
\end{equation}

\par Now, if a substitution is applied with the Gladstone-Dale relationship, one can obtain:

\begin{equation}
\label{equation:epsilon-nabla rho}
    \vec{\varepsilon}
=
\frac{K}{n_0}
\int \nabla \rho \, dz
\end{equation}

\par Equation \ref{equation:epsilon-nabla rho} directly links the light deviation vector to the density gradient through the medium. Thus, in order to obtain a quantitative measure of the density field through a test section, one could employ a method to measure the displacement of light after passing through it. If the transversal refractive gradients are independent of the line of sight direction, such as a 2D planar flow, Equation \ref{equation:epsilon-nabla rho} can be simplified into Equation \ref{equation: Displacement and Density Gradient} for $y$, where $W$ is the refractive object thickness \cite{RajendranDotTracking}. This equation will be used in the final experimental campaign of this work to compute the density gradient of a 2D planar flow through a linear aerospike nozzle.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Displacement and Density Gradient}
    \varepsilon_y = \frac{K\cdot W}{n_0} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial y}
\end{equation}

\par The equation shows that light bends in the direction of the density gradient, that is, the direction of highest density. For a 2D planar flow, if the displacements caused by light passing through it are measured in a plane, the object density gradient can be computed and integrated to obtain a density field. For a more complex 3D flow, multiple lines of sight must be used to reconstruct a tomographic image of the density. However, some 3D flows can be reconstructed with only a 2D view due to their symmetry. An axisymmetric body such a circular compressible jet is such a case. The next section will demonstrate how axisymmetry can be used to obtain the 3D density field using 2D displacement vectors. The theory will be used later to obtain a quantitative density field of a compressible under-expanded jet.

\section{Axisymmetric Refractive Field}
\label{subsection: Axisymmetric Refractive Field}

\par When a refractive object is axially symmetric, it is possible to reconstruct its whole density distribution with only a 2D snapshot of the light displacements through it. In such a scenario, the image is a 2D projection of a 3D axisymmetric body, and the inverse Abel transform can be used to obtain its radial and axial profile \cite{Abel1826}. Consider the case in Figure \ref{fig: Abel transform} and Equation \ref{equation: displacement integral} for $y$; it can be integrated in $y$ on both sides into Equation \ref{equation: integral of epsilon}, which removes the partial derivative through the fundamental theorem of calculus. There, $E_y$ is the integral in $y$ of the deflection $\varepsilon_y$ and $R$ is the outer radius of the refractive object, beyond which the refractive index returns to its ambient value $n_0$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{figures/AbelTransform.png}
    \caption[Geometry of the Abel transform for an axisymmetric body]{Diagram of the geometry of an axisymmetric body being seen by an observer in I. If the body, in grey, is described by a function $f(r)$, the observer will see its projection $F(y)$, which is its Abel transform}
    \label{fig: Abel transform}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: integral of epsilon}
    E_y = \frac{2}{n_0}\int_0^{\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}} [n(r) - n_0]\,dz
\end{equation}

\par Now, by changing $z$ for the radius $r$ through the relation $z = \sqrt{r^2 - y^2}$, so that the lower limit of integration becomes $r = y$, $E_y$ becomes the plain Abel transform of the refractive indexes $n - n_0$ in Equation \ref{equation: Abel transform of n}. This means that when the inverse Abel transformation is applied to $E_y$, a radial profile of $n$ is obtained, which can be translated to the density through the Gladstone-Dale relation. The principles described in this section will be used later to obtain the quantitative density field of an axisymetric jet by aplying the abel inversion computational method described by Daun \cite{Daun2006}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Abel transform of n}
    E_y = \frac{2}{n_0} \int_y^R \frac{[n(r) - n_0]}{\sqrt{r^2-y^2}}\,r\,dr
\end{equation}

It is the ability to relate optical distortions to density gradients that forms the foundation of schlieren-based diagnostic techniques.
