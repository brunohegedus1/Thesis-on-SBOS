\chapter{Results}
\label{chapter: Results}

Chapter \ref{chapter: Results} presents the results obtained with the experimental setups described in Chapter \ref{chapter: Experimental Setups}. The data collected was processed, its associated uncertainty quantified and analyzed, an interpretation of the results was supplied and their implication to the research questions discussed. In total, 4 experiments were conducted which allowed a comprehensive study of each of the 3 main aspects of the SBOS technique, namely its optical configuration, speckle pattern formation and schlieren object, to finally implement the method in a wind tunnel setting.

The first experiment imaged a simple refractive object with the goal of comparing different optical configurations and validating the design guidelines presented in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}. The second campaign tackled the speckle formation and studied the parameters that affect it as reported by the literature. When a sufficient speckle pattern was obtained, an under-expanded jet was imaged using SBOS. The displacement data collected were then turned into quantitative density fields and validated against literature. The lessons learned in each of these applications culminated in the final implementation of SBOS in the study of the flow through a linear aerospike nozzle in a high-speed wind tunnel. The raw displacements were processed into density gradients that allowed the interpretation of compression and expansion structures. These results were then compared and validated against traditional schlieren images of the same flow.

Together with Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}, this chapter groups the information necessary to answer the research questions posed in Chapter \ref{chapter: introduction}. It experimentally compares SBOS to different optical density measurement techniques such as standard BOS and traditional schlieren, presents the main uncertainty values associated with the methods used, investigates the implications of having the focus inside the test section, and describes the challenges of implementing the method in a wind-tunnel environment. In doing so, it also demonstrates the capabilities and limitations of this infant technique, showing its enormous potential to numerous applications.

\subsection{Uncertainty Budgets}
\label{subsection: Angled Glass Uncertainty Budgets}

\par Tables \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} and \ref{tab: sensitivity comparison} both show total uncertainties that were obtained to better assess the measurement error. These values were computed through uncertainty budgets following the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement \cite{JCGM2008}. The campaign compared a displacement obtained by Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass} against the displacement measured by cross-correlation. Therefore, two budgets were constructed, one for the glass displacement and another for the CC measurement. Afterwards, the collected uncertainties were combined to obtain the sensitivity uncertainty. A description of each budget and the formulas used to assess the total contribution follows.

The glass displacement's uncertainty budget is summarized in Table \ref{tab: angled glass uncertainty}. It focuses on the three inputs of the angled glass displacement estimate, namely the plate thickness $T$, the plate angle $\theta$ and the refractive index $n$. Thickness was measured with a caliper and the angle was set with a protractor, while the refractive index was obtained from the literature for common glass. Each input was treated as a rectangular probability distribution of half-width $a$ with a standard uncertainty of $u = a/\sqrt{3}$. The sensitivity coefficients and contributions of each input variable regarding the two glass angles were also estimated based on Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass}. These coefficients measure how much a change in each variable affects the final displacement. They are also used to calculate the contribution of each variable to the combined uncertainty as it is equal to the sensitivity coefficient times uncertainty \cite{JCGM2008}. These quantities are presented in Table \ref{tab: angled glass uncertainty}, where the half-width and the standard uncertainty are listed for every input, followed by its contribution at each glass angle. The combined uncertainty for each angle is given at the bottom row and corresponds to the root of the sum of squares of the above percentages.

\subsection{Speckle Size Discrepancy}
\label{subsection: Angled Glass Speckle Size}

Section \ref{section: Angled Glass Experiment} mentioned a discrepancy between the speckle size obtained through the Rayleigh diffraction limit and the ones obtained experimentally and computed using Hamarová's method \cite{Hamarova2016}. Table \ref{tab: angled glass speckle results} displays both quantities side by side together with the $f_\#$ and $M_{ag}$ used at each point. The $\Delta s$ deviation shows that at some points the discrepancy exceeds the theoretical value several times. The data gives some clues on what has happened and what could be improved for a next experiment.

From Table \ref{tab: angled glass speckle results}, it seems that the f-stop number did not influence the average speckle size obtained experimentally. This becomes evident from observing points 4 and 8--10. In these points, the $f_\#$ ranges from 4 to 16 whereas $\Delta s$ stays within 12.35 px to 12.94 px. In contrast, changes in magnification were observed to have an effect in the experimental average speckle size considerably. From points 1--4 the $f_\#$ is fixed and $M_{ag}$ changes from 0.671 to 1.045, displaying $\Delta s$ from 12.74 px to 21.21 px. These factors contradict the Rayleigh equation for subjective speckles where its size is only dependent on the light's wavelength, imaging lens $f_\#$ and $M_{ag}$. The independence to the aperture size indicates that the interference pattern is not being created at the lens pupil as it should \cite{Goodman2020}. The magnification effect, in turn, means that the speckle is behaving like an imaged object. These factors lead to the belief that the pattern did not form a Rayleigh distribution at the lens pupil, rather what is being imaged is the interference pattern becoming diffraction limited at the diffuser, which might be caused by the small diameter of the beam when it hits the ground glass.

\subsection{F-stop and Magnification Dependence}
\label{subsection: Speckle Aperture and Magnification}

Now that the error and uncertainties of the results have been discussed, the data can be plotted to better understand its behavior, such as f-stop dependency. The various $f_\#$ sweeps made during the experimental campaign allow the evaluation of whether the interference waves became diffraction limited at the pupil. Figure \ref{fig: speckle f-number sweep} shows the trend of average speckle size with f-stop number for four different configurations. All lines follow the characteristic increasing trend of a developed subjective speckle, which follows Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit}, and signals that the interference front is becoming diffraction limited at the lens pupil \cite{Goodman2020}. The figure shows some promising trends as there is a clear dependence on $f_\#$ and it indicates a tendency of projected speckles to be smaller than direct ones. However, it also shows an insensitivity to the optical magnification, contradicting Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit}, and the effect of $d_{GG}$. These trends will be further discussed next.

In order to directly compare the projected speckle result with directed speckles, some values had to be interpolated using the collected data. The goal was to find 3 points at $d_{GG} = 300$ mm, $M_{ag} = 0.457$ and $f_\# = 4, 11$ and $22$. First, points 20 and 13 were used to obtain $\Delta_s = 3.67$ px for an f-stop = 22. Then, points 8, 5 and 2 gave an estimate to f-stop = 11 equals to $2.47$ px. Lastly, points 1 and 4 are used to obtain a $M_{ag}$ slope which is applied to point 11 to obtain an estimate $\Delta_s = 2.34$ px at f-stop = 4. The line in blue displays these points in Figure \ref{fig: speckle f-number sweep}, while the line in green shows the projected speckle trend. Comparing both trends, the Figure demonstrates that projected speckles are generally smaller than the directed speckles. The conclusion maintains, however, that the results are too similar to the direct speckle approach and the decision of which generation method to choose comes down to practical aspects rather than speckle quality.

The magnification effect can be assessed through the comparison of the red and yellow lines in Figure \ref{fig: speckle f-number sweep}, as the two configurations only vary in magnification. It appears that the increased magnification of the red line caused practically no effect in the average speckle size, displaying almost the same values as the yellow line. The same insensitivity to magnification was also experienced through points 14--28 shown in Figure \ref{fig: speckle magnification sweep}, where magnification was swept by modifying the focal distance through signaled by marks of the camera lens. This method of defining the distance to the focal plane is less accurate than the calibration method used in the other points, so it is to be expected that the magnification figure in these points is less accurate.  Nevertheless, the figure shows, for a different $d_{GG}$, the same speckle size insensitivity to optical magnification that was identified in Figure \ref{fig: speckle f-number sweep}, which effectively contradicts the Rayleigh diffraction limit of Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit}. The cause of this contradiction might come from the violation of one fundamental assumption used to derive the equation for subjective speckles.

As explained in chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}, the dependency on magnification in Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit} comes from the thin lens assumption \cite{NikitaFominBook}. Where it was shown that Two types of speckle formation schemes exist: objective and subjective. Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh limit} is dericed based on the Rayleigh limit for objective speckles and the thin lens equation used to calculate $Z$. However, since a compound camera is being used to image the speckles in this work, the distance between the lens and the image is not the same as it would be for a thin lens and the substitution does not hold. Therefore, another equation must exist that better describes the behavior of speckles for a compound lens. Such equation must maintain the dependency to the lens aperture to remain consisted to the results but rely on another effective $Z$ that takes into account the compound character of the camera.

\subsection{Effect of the Beam Diameter at the Diffuser}
\label{subsection: Speckle Beam Diameter}

Figure \ref{fig: speckle f-number sweep} also hints to the effect of the distance between the ground glass and the beam expansion lens $d_{GG}$ in the speckle generation. By comparing the purple line with red and yellow, it appears that a lower distance increased the speckle pattern, with purple exceeding red by 0.68, 0.25 and 0.32 px for $f_\#$ = 4, 11.31 and 22.63, respectively, although the difference is only subpixel. The blue line seems smaller than yellow, even though it has a supposedly higher magnification, but that could be due to errors in the interpolation. To better assess the effect of $d_{GG}$ and consequently the beam diameter that hits the diffuser, Figure \ref{fig: speckle dGG sweep} displays the data obtained by varying only the distance between the ground glass and the expanding optics.

The beam was expanded through a convergent lens, meaning that the distance between the lens and the ground glass diffuser is a proxy for the beam diameter. Therefore, it is possible to verify the influence of the beam diameter in the speckle generation by plotting the points where only $d_{GG}$ was varied. Figure \ref{fig: speckle dGG sweep} shows that the average speckle size remains consistent for high $d_{GG}$, as it varies from 2.91 px at 190 mm, dips to 2.29 px at 450 mm and goes to 2.71 px at 740 mm. This variation is coherent with the findings of Figure \ref{fig: speckle f-number sweep}. It demonstrates that $d_{GG}$ can increase the speckle size but for large distances the spread is practically subpixel. However, the figure also shows a spike at low $d_{GG}$ when the beam diameter is small, reaching 4.59 px at 100 mm which yields a beam diameter equal to 25\% of the original. The DaVis function even failed to compute a value for this point because the illuminated envelope was too small and the speckles too large. That indicates that a sufficiently small beam diameter hitting the diffuser can influence greatly the speckle formation.

In the angled glass study, it was mentioned that a probable cause of the large speckles reported during the campaign could be the laser beam becoming diffraction limited at the diffuser instead of the lens pupil. The data supports this hypothesis, indicating that the small diameter of the laser when it hit the 1.27 cm ground glass diffuser indeed could be the cause of larger speckles. The effect of different beam diameters hitting the ground glass can be better visualized through Figure \ref{fig: speckle patterns dGG} which displays side-by-side the captured speckle patterns for points 5, 8 and 10 respectively. The image shows that, despite the difference in diameter between them, there seems to be little difference between the speckle size of Point 5 and Point 8, which indicates that at those regimes the light is still becoming diffraction limited at the lens pupil. However, Point 10 seems to have a completely different structure, much brighter than the previous ones with much less discernible speckles, at that point it is believed that the diffraction limit is reached at the diffuser. This means that, when designing the laser optical setup, an experimentalist must worry about expanding the laser beam enough before it hits the ground glass, so it is diffraction limited at the lens pupil and capable of filling the desired field of view.

The speckle pattern investigation campaign allowed for the study of the speckle generation process. It successfully reproduced finer speckles than the angled glass experiment, showed the dependence of the speckle size on the f-stop number, investigated the effect of a small incidence beam in the diffuser and compared direct with projected speckle. Therefore, it was conjectured that another relationship different than Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit} must exist for compound lenses. For now, the data at hand makes it possible to define a laser speckle generator capable of producing speckles within the 2--5 pixel range. The findings of this campaign are enough to proceed to the correct application of Laser Speckled BOS to image a real schlieren object, in this case a compressible jet.

The strategy envisioned to better estimate the nozzle pressure ratio builds on top of the definition of the Prandtl-Meyer angle $\nu$. As explained in Section \ref{section: Compressibility}, $\nu$ is the turning angle needed for a flow at a certain Mach number $M_1$ to expand to a Mach number $M_2$. In the case studied, the flow is choked at the nozzle exit, so the Mach number at the exit is assumed to be equal to one $M_e = 1$. At the lip, the flow expands isentropically until it reaches a certain $M_j$ at ambient pressure. Therefore, the angle of the flow at the shear layer is equal to $\varphi = \nu(M_j)$ relative to the jet axis. To obtain a better estimate of the $NPR$, the images were treated to make the jet axis horizontal, then the shear layer angle at the lip was measured, $M_j$ was calculated through the Prandtl-Meyer function and a value for $p_0$ was obtained using isentropic expansion. The values can be found in Table \ref{tab: compressible jet NPR estimates} together with the uncertainty corresponding to $+ 1^\circ$ in $\nu$.

The density gradient field was obtained using the displacements in the focal plane assuming a planar field, through Equation \ref{equation: In-plane displacement} \cite{Dalziel2000, vanHinsberg2014}. This result corresponds to the projection of the axisymmetric body. In reality, the thickness of the object $W$ and the density gradient vary with the radius, so this solution will underestimate the density gradients at the borders of the jet. Nevertheless, it is useful to qualitatively study the shock cell structure, as the periodicity of the cell and the gradient signs are maintained in the projection. So, it was assumed that the deflection angle comes from the midplane of the jet and an effective lever arm was applied $l_{eff} = l+W/2$ to compute the density gradients. Figure \ref{fig: dataset f density gradients} shows the gradients for dataset f in the two directions s and n together with the magnitude of the vector. For this application, the background of the image was removed by calculating the median vector of a window only containing the background, then the image was rotated and translated such that the jet axis is horizontal and corresponds to $n = 0$.

\begin{equation}
\label{equation: In-plane displacement}
    \Delta \xi = \frac{G\cdot W\cdot l_{eff}}{n_0}\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial x}
\end{equation}

After the density peaks, a new round of expansion waves causes the density to decrease, thus starting a new shock cell. \subsection{Quantitative Density Field}
\label{subsection: Quantitative Density Field results}

One of the goals of the compressible jet experiment was the quantitative reproduction of its density field using the displacement data gathered with SBOS. To achieve that, the vector field had to go through a series of post-processing steps, integration schemes and noise regularization. The first ones were already described: the median of the background vector was removed and the vectors were translated and rotated to position the jet axis horizontally at $n = 0$. The two halves of the field were then folded about $n = 0$ and averaged. The streamwise displacement is an even function of $n$, so the two halves were summed and halved, while the normal displacement is an odd function, so the lower half was multiplied by $-1$ before the average was taken. The correct positioning of the axis is essential for this step and for the Abel inversion, as both assume an axisymmetric field and any misalignment induces errors by mixing the two halves of the jet.

Next, the folded normal displacement was divided by the effective lever arm $l + W/2$, with $l = 30$ mm and $W = 4$ mm, to obtain the deflection angle through Equation \ref{equation: S BOS}. Then, following Section \ref{subsection: Axisymmetric Refractive Field}, the deflection was first integrated in $y$ from the freestream towards the jet axis, which yields $E_y$, the Abel transform of $n - n_0$ in Equation \ref{equation: Abel transform of n}. The inverse transform was computed with the onion-peeling method of Dasch \cite{Dasch1992}, which divides the jet into concentric rings of constant refractive index. Onion-peeling converges to the exact profile for noise-free data. However, if noise is present, it accumulates errors from the outer rings inwards and produces large deviations at the centerline. To mitigate this effect, the inversion was solved with a Tikhonov regularization, as proposed by Daun et al. \cite{Daun2006}. The regularization parameter was chosen for each dataset by Generalized Cross-Validation \cite{Golub1979}. Finally, the refractive index was converted into density through the Gladstone-Dale relation, with $G = 2.26 \times 10^{-4}$ m$^3$/kg and an ambient density of $1.2$ kg/m$^3$. The resultant density fields of datasets a to f are displayed in Figure \ref{fig: density fields jets}.

Through analysis of Figure \ref{fig: centerline density e vs panda}, dataset e can be compared with the data from literature to validate the density field results. The plot shows that the SBOS data closely fits the first valley and peak values of Panda and Seasholtz \cite{Panda1999}, with the peaks presenting less than 1\% of deviation from the literature and the valleys diverging by less than 5\%. However, the data starts to drift from the second cell downstream, where the literature shock cells shrink and decay in amplitude much faster. The shock cell length of the SBOS measurements has already been validated with the literature data from Hartmann-Lazarus, so another effect must be playing a part. It was conjectured that this discrepancy can be explained by the size difference between the nozzle of Panda and Seasholtz and the one employed in this experiment. The bigger jet disturbs more air, causing turbulence and a thicker shear layer, which increases the jet energy dissipation with ambient air and accelerates the decay of the shock cell length, an effect of the Reynolds number. This effect is smaller at the beginning of the jet and increases with length, therefore, the match of the first cell is enough to validate the results obtained. Nevertheless, it is worth explaining the discrepancy between the two plots.

% REVIEW: check that Ozawa et al. state Re = 10^5 as the laminar-turbulent transition. In the next paragraph, the Hartmann-Lazarus range Re = 2.7 to 4.0 x 10^5 is slightly above an estimate at NPR = 4 (about 2.4 to 3.6 x 10^5); state the NPR assumed.
The Reynolds number can be used to assess the development of a boundary layer and infer if the flow is turbulent or laminar. The number described in Equation \ref{equation: Reynolds Number} is proportional to the nozzle diameter $d_N$, velocity $V$ and density $\rho$, and inversely proportional to the viscosity $\mu$. Using the fully expanded values obtained with isentropic relations, it is possible to compare the Reynolds number of this work with the one of Panda and Seasholtz \cite{Panda1999}. Since $M_j$ is the same, the Reynolds number scales with only $d_N$. For this work's $d_N = 3.5$ mm and $M_j = 1.43$, Re = $1.78 \cdot 10^5$, while for the $d_N = 25.4$ mm of Panda and Seasholtz, $Re = 1.29 \cdot 10^6$. This difference of a factor of 7 is significant in this conditions as $Re \approx 10^5$ marks the transitional regime between a partially laminar and a fully turbulent jet \cite{Ozawa2020}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: Reynolds Number}
    Re = \frac{\rho Vd_N}{\mu}
\end{equation}

The jets in the transition regime between laminar and turbulent jets experience large differences in the boundary layer shape, as investigated by Ozawa et al. \cite{Ozawa2020}. Figure 12 of their work shows that, for larger Reynolds number (Re = $10^6$), the velocity along the centerline decays faster, while it stays more persistently high at low Reynolds ($Re = 10^5$), meaning that higher Reynolds effects do indeed increase decay downstream. Further literature agrees with these findings. Seiner and Norum \cite{Seiner1980} have reported that the spread of the viscous mixing layer shifts the shock positions predicted by inviscid solutions and weakens the shock strength further downstream. Moreover, the fact that Hartmann-Lazarus formulation matched this work's data can also be explained by Reynolds similarity as the range of $M_j$ was similar ($NPR$ around 4) and their nozzle diameter ranged from 4 mm to 6 mm, yielding a $Re \approx 2.7$--$4.0 \cdot 10^5$ \cite{Hartmann1941}.

% REVIEW: "The density results were validated against the centerline density data of Panda and Seasholtz" overstates the comparison, which matched the first cell only.
The compressible jet experiments allowed the imaging of a real schlieren object using Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren, resulting in a displacement field which was later processed into quantitative density data. The CC results yielded a high spatial resolution, which made possible the identification of the small jet's shock cell structural features and the comparison between different $NPR$s. The shock cell lengths were compared to literature data from Hartmann-Lazarus and Emden \cite{Hartmann1941, Emden1899, Powell2010}, validating the $NPR$ estimates made by measuring the shear layer angle at the nozzle lip. Abel inversion with Tikhonov regularization was then implemented to obtain quantitative density fields of the jets \cite{Abel1826,Dasch1992, Daun2006, Golub1979}. The density results were validated against the centerline density data of Panda and Seasholtz \cite{Panda1999}. The unusually small diameter of the nozzle used also permitted the visualization of Reynolds effects when compared to the larger exit diameters common in the study of under-expanded jets. Therefore, the experiment shows that SBOS can be used to image small flows with high resolution and to extract meaningful quantitative data using a relatively simple setup.

\subsection{In-Focus Sensitivity from Displacement Ratio}
\label{subsection: Aerospike S Ratio}

Nevertheless, imaging the flow inside the test section comes with its own limitations. Since there is no defocus distance $l$, it means that the sensitivity cannot be estimated as $S=l \cdot M_{ag}$, which breaks the current model on how to transform the measured displacement field into density gradients and subsequently a quantitative density field. A solution for that lies in Equation \ref{equation: S BOS}, as the sensitivity is also defined as the ratio between the measured displacement and deflection induced by the schlieren object $S = \Delta/\varepsilon$. If the same object of study is imaged with different sensitivities, the deflection $\varepsilon$ will be the same but the displacement measured will be different. The ratio between the measured displacements will be the ratio of the sensitivities. Therefore, if a refractive object is imaged with a certain known sensitivity, a second sensitivity can be estimated by using the ratio of the measured displacements. Equation \ref{equation: S ratio} summarizes this formulation.

\begin{equation}
    \label{equation: S ratio}
    S_2 = \frac{\Delta_2}{\Delta_1} S_1
\end{equation}

The maximum measured displacement of points 1, 2 and 3 were taken from the Cross-Correlation result and displayed in Table \ref{tab: S ratio}. The sensitivity of point 1 was calculated based on the one of point 3 and vice-versa in the ratio method column. Then, the sensitivity of point 2 was computed based on points 1 and 3. The deviation column shows that this method matches remarkably well the  $S= l \cdot M_{ag}$ formulation for the two defocused points, with less than 5\% of error. For the point inside the test section, the sensitivity estimate using either defocused point also yield similar results with less than 5\% of difference. These deviations between the two sensitivity estimates might come from errors in the schlieren object such as $NPR$ fluctuations, that affect $\varepsilon$ and $\Delta_{max}$, or in the magnification and distance measurements. Nevertheless, this small demonstration shows the simplicity and promise of this method, enabling SBOS images to be taken with the refractive object in focus.

For that, point 8 was selected and its gradients, displayed in Figure \ref{fig: aerospike density gradient point 8}, were carefully analyzed to produce Figure \ref{fig: aerospike flow interpretation point 8}. The same color convention applies, with red for a positive gradient and blue for a negative one. A striking red line of positive gradient next to the exhaust in the x component marks a compression wave. This wave reflects at the ramp into another compression wave signaled by a strong blue line of negative gradient in the y component and goes to the shear layer. In the shear layer, the wave is reflected into expansion fans, marked by a continuous blue zone of negative gradient in the x component. At the tip, a strong blue line of negative gradient in the y component and faint red dots of positive gradient in x mark a compression wave, which is followed below by an expansion wave signaled by a strong red line of positive gradient in y. There was no schlieren image available for comparison with the SBOS result with the same $NPR = 8$, only for $NPR = 7$. Nevertheless, the comparison is valid, as it is possible to see that the structures identified in SBOS match the schlieren image and are only shifted slightly to the left due to the lower $NPR$.

% REVIEW: the text says the flow changed at NPR > 14, but the highest point in the sweep is point 11 at NPR = 14, which is the one said to show the standing shock.
The effect of $NPR$ in the flow's structures of the linear aerospike can be assessed by plotting the density gradients of points 8--11, as done in Figure \ref{fig: aerospike density gradients NPR sweep}. Comparing the four plot pairs, it becomes evident that an increase in nozzle pressure ratio causes the initial compression wave to extend further downstream and the reflections to shift to the right, similar to the trend documented for the free jet. Another notable observation is that the expansion fan in the tip starts to point more sharply down with increasing $NPR$, indicating the tendency to close the truncation base flow as mentioned by Wisse \cite{Wisse2005}. Unfortunately, the effect could not be observed, as the flow changed dramatically at $NPR > 14$ impeding the closure of the base flow, as shown by Figure \ref{fig: aerospike density gradient point 11}. The cause of the change was the absence of a model extension which created a low pressure zone at the end of the model. The difference between the mounted model and the designed one can be seen in Figures \ref{fig: aerospike model} and \ref{fig: aerospike technical drawing}. With the increase in $NPR$, the pressure difference between the nozzle exit and the low pressure zone grew, until a standing shock was formed at the ramp's edge. The discrepancy between point 7 and 11 can be explained by this. Point 11 was probably captured at a larger $NPR$ than point 7, so the shock was formed in the former, while the same did not happen for the latter.

The aerospike experiment implemented the SBOS method in a high-speed wind tunnel environment, showcasing the challenges and strengths of the technique in a complex experimental setting. The distances prescribed by the design methodology of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} could not be exactly replicated due to the behavior of the compound camera lens. Nevertheless, the resulting setup performance was not much different from the selected one and it set a good starting point for further tweaking at the test site, which demonstrates the usefulness of the methodology as a preliminary design tool. The results allowed the comparison of different defocus configurations, highlighting the unique SBOS capability of dynamically changing the sensitivity and resolution through simple modifications in the optical setup. The section also demonstrated that SBOS can be successfully applied with the camera focus inside the test section by using the S ratio method developed and validated by this work. In-focus SBOS provides remarkable spatial resolution, capable of rivaling even the traditional and well-established schlieren technique. The raw displacement fields obtained were then processed into density gradient fields that permitted a qualitative interpretation of the flow's compression and expansion structures. This qualitative analysis can be easily turned into a quantitative result through the integration of the density gradient field. Furthermore, the method was able to capture physical trends across an $NPR$ sweep, showing the initial compression wave extending further downstream and the tip expansion fan turning more sharply with increasing nozzle pressure ratio, consistent with the free jet trend and Wisse \cite{Wisse2005}. Therefore, the experiment demonstrates that SBOS can be successfully applied to a supersonic wind tunnel using a very simple optical arrangement and a low power laser. Finally, it establishes SBOS as a method capable of reproducing resolutions and sensitivities comparable to traditional schlieren while still providing the ease of quantitative measurements present in BOS.

The four experimental campaigns described in this chapter gathered the necessary data to answer the research question and sub-questions posed in Chapter \ref{chapter: introduction}. The first experiment compared different defocus configurations obtained with the design methodology of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}. It showed that negative defocus SBOS is capable of reaching higher magnifications than standard BOS, which allows it to reach a better sensitivity-resolution trade-off. The reproduction of the trends present in the design surface maps also validated the methodology. The speckle pattern investigation tried to verify the effect of certain parameters on the average speckle size reported in the literature \cite{Barker&Fourney, NikitaFominBook, Meier&Roesgen2013}. It confirmed the dependency of the speckle formation on the lens aperture and the size of the beam diameter as it hits the diffuser but reported an insensitivity to the magnification, which led to the conjecture that the Rayleigh limit cannot be used for compound lenses in the format of Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit}. Its findings enabled the construction of a laser speckle generator capable of reproducing speckles in the 2--5 pixel range, as recommended by the literature. With this pattern, the compressible jet experiment converted a measured displacement field from SBOS into quantitative density fields, that were validated against the shock cell correlations of Hartmann-Lazarus \cite{Hartmann1941} and against the centerline density of Panda and Seasholtz \cite{Panda1999}. At last, the aerospike campaign successfully implemented the method in the ST-15 wind tunnel, introduced the possibility of imaging with the refractive object in focus and compared the results against traditional schlieren of the same flow. Nevertheless, the experiment also exposed the limitations of the technique, especially the blur decorrelation suffered by the defocused configurations in flows with strong density gradients and turbulence. These results, together with the recommendations for future work, will be used to answer the research questions in Chapter \ref{chapter: Conclusion}.
