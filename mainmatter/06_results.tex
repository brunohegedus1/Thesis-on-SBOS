\section{Angled Glass Experiment Results}

The Angled Glass Experiment aimed to validate the methodology and compare the different defocus configurations treated in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}. This section will display the data that has been collected, present how it was treated, discuss the uncertainty of the results and conclude whether these objectives have been achieved. The tables that follow give an overview of the results.

Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} compares the displacements measured through SBOS with the predicted shift induced by the angled glass. $\Delta_x$ is the mean of the cross-correlation vector field in pixels, $\Delta x_{SBOS}$ is the latter transferred to mm and $\Delta x_{glass}$ is the glass-induced ray shift, calculated through Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass}. Since the glass piece produces a uniform displacement field, the standard deviation of the cross-correlation field $\sigma_\Delta$ quantifies the precision of the SBOS measurement, while the relative error quantifies the accuracy of the method. All errors are below 10\%, with most of them below 4\% and $\sigma_\Delta$ also shows small values. The largest deviation was point 1 with a relative error of 7.87 \%. The optical magnification and $d_f$ error shown in Table \ref{tab: df and Mag angled glass results} do not stand out for point 1 compared to the other points, so the design values alone do not reveal the source of that deviation. Subsection \ref{subsection: angled glass uncertainty} weighs this point against the uncertainty of the campaign and treats it in more detail. Nevertheless, these metrics indicate that the technique has been well implemented and is capable of producing accurate and precise results.

Through the data collected during the experimental campaign, it is possible to obtain sensitivity through two experimental routes, each one provides a different insight on the error sources and validity of the experiment. Table \ref{tab: sensitivity comparison} compares the theoretical sensitivity given by the surface map of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} to sensitivity obtained through the geometry $S_{geom} = l \cdot M_{ag_{exp}}$ and the sensitivity obtained with the displacement data $S_\Delta = \Delta_x \cdot l_{px}/\varepsilon_{glass}$. With the exception of point 7, which has already been discussed, all data is within 8\% of agreement. It is worth noting that the $S_{geom}$ error is almost the same as the magnification error, while $S_\Delta$ stacks both the displacement measurement error and the geometric setup error. The data also validates the methodology of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} as it demonstrates that the physical setup reproduces the predicted performance.

With the data at hand it is also possible to compare the performance of a setup that uses negative defocus to another with positive defocus, effectively comparing the new capacities of SBOS to a standard BOS configuration. Figure \ref{fig: S vs l experimental} plots the sensitivity versus defocus distance of points 1 to 9 that were captured with $f=200$ mm, with error bars corresponding to the standard deviation divided by the mean cross-correlation displacement. The plot reproduces the findings of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}, where for a given defocus distance $l$, sensitivity is higher if the distance is negative.

Points 10 to 20 of Tables \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} and \ref{tab: sensitivity comparison} show the results of the experiment where the angled glass was positioned in a linear slider with a fixed magnification and only the defocus position was changed. Figure \ref{fig: S vs l slider experiment} plots the resulting sensitivity varied with defocus distance. The points are symmetric around the Y-axis, which means that the mechanism that makes a negative defocus configuration more powerful than its positive counterpart is not the negative distance per se but the increased magnification that comes with a shorter focal distance to the camera. Although the resolution has not been measured in this case, it is also to be expected through Equation \ref{equation: S over smin} that the spatial resolution trade-off is improved by this increase in magnification.

\par To better assess and interpret the deviations of the Tables above, uncertainty budgets were performed. The campaign compares a displacement predicted by Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass} against a displacement measured by cross-correlation. Each side of the equation carries its own uncertainty, so two budgets were  constructed based on the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement \cite{JCGM2008}. The first budget focuses on the three inputs of the angled glass displacement prediction, namely the plate thickness $T$, the plate angle $\theta$ and the refractive index $n$. Thickness was measured with a caliper and the angle was set with a protractor, while the refractive index was obtained from the literature as the value for common glass. Each input was treated as a rectangular probability distribution of half-width $a$ with a standard uncertainty of $u = a/\sqrt{3}$. The sensitivity coefficients and contributions of each input variable regarding the two glass angles were also estimated based on Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass}. All of these quantities are collected in Table \ref{tab: angled glass uncertainty}, where the half-width and the standard uncertainty are listed for every input, followed by its contribution at each glass angle. The contribution of a variable corresponds to its sensitivity coefficient times its uncertainty, and the combined uncertainty for each angle is the root of the sum of squares of the above percentages, given at the bottom of the table.

\par Regarding the cross-correlation displacement measurement, it is affected mainly by two variables. The first is the scatter of the cross-correlation vector field $\sigma_\Delta$, listed in Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results}. It runs from 2.0\% to 6.0\% of the mean displacement with an average of 3.2\%. This term is evaluated from the vector field itself and therefore varies from point to point. The second is the optical magnification returned by the DaVis calibration. The calibration fit residual stayed within 2.9 px over the sensor width of 2560 px, which means a contribution of only 0.07\%, if a rectangular distribution is assumed. The pixel pitch  $l_{px}$ is fixed by the manufacturer and is negligible against variables. Table \ref{tab: measurement uncertainty} presents the second budget.

\par This budget allows for better judgment of the deviations of Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results}. Every point except point 1 falls within its own standard uncertainty, and points 17 and 18 are the only ones that require the interval defined by $k = 2$. The agreement is therefore better than the raw percentages suggest, since the points with the largest deviations are also the ones with the largest scatter. Point 1 is the exception at 7.87\%, which is 2.5 times its combined standard uncertainty of 3.11\%. The glass angle alone does not explain it, because an error of $1.26^{\circ}$ would be required and that value exceeds the reading uncertainty of the protractor by more than a factor of four. The most likely explanation is a combination of a misread angle and a plate that was not seated flat against its mount. %Improve this paragraph... somehow

\par The eleven slider points deserve a separate remark. Each one falls within its own uncertainty, however all of them deviate in the same direction and none of them shows any dependence on $l$. A one-signed offset of this kind indicates a systematic error rather than scatter, and an angle offset of $0.57^{\circ}$ reproduces it exactly. Those points share a single setting of the glass plate, so a small constant error in $\theta$ is the most plausible source. This also confirms that the measured displacement is independent of the defocus distance, as anticipated in Chapter \ref{chapter: Experimental Setups}.
