\section{Angled Glass Experiment Results}

The Angled Glass Experiment aimed to validate the methodology and compare the different defocus configurations treated in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}. This section will display the data that has been collected, present how it was treated and discuss whether these objectives have been achieved. The tables that follow give an overview of the results.

Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} compares the displacements measured through SBOS with the predicted shift induced by the angled glass. $\Delta_x$ is the mean of the cross-correlation vector field in pixels, $\Delta x_{SBOS}$ is the latter transferred to mm through $\rho_{px}$ and $\Delta x_{glass}$ is the glass-induced ray shift, calculated through Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass}. Since the glass piece produces a uniform displacement field, the standard deviation of the cross-correlation field $\sigma_\Delta$ quantifies the precision of the SBOS measurement, while the relative error quantifies the accuracy of the method. All errors are below 10\%, with most of them below 4\% and $\sigma_\Delta$ also shows small values. The only outlier was point 1 with a relative error of 7.87 \%. Given that the optical magnification and $d_f$ error shown in Table \ref{tab: df and Mag angled glass results} do not stand out for point 1 compared to other points, most probably the source of the error was in the measurement of the glass angle for that point. Nevertheless, these metrics indicate that the technique has been well implemented and is capable of producing accurate and precise results.

Through the data collected during the experimental campaign, it is possible to obtain sensitivity through two experimental routes and each gives a different insight on the error sources and validity of the experiment. Table \ref{tab: sensitivity comparison} compares the theoretical sensitivity given by the surface map of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} to sensitivity obtained through the geometry $S_{geom} = l \cdot M_{ag_{exp}}$ and the sensitivity obtained with the displacement data $S_\Delta = \Delta_x \cdot l_{px}/\varepsilon_{glass}$. With the exception of point 7, which has already been treated, all data is within 8\% of agreement. It is worth noting that the $S_{geom}$ error is almost the same as the magnification error, while $S_\Delta$ stacks both the displacement measurement error and the geometric setup error. The data also validates the methodology of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} as it demonstrates that the physical setup reproduces the predicted performance.

With the data at hand it is also possible to compare the performance of a setup that uses negative defocus to another with positive defocus, effectively comparing the new capacities of SBOS to a standard BOS configuration. Figure \ref{fig: S vs l experimental} plots the sensitivity versus defocus distance of points 1 to 9 that were captured with $f=200$ mm, with error bars corresponding to the standard deviation divided by the mean cross-correlation displacement. The plot reproduces the findings of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}, where for a given defocus distance $l$, sensitivity is higher if the distance is negative.

Points 10 to 20 of Tables \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} and \ref{tab: sensitivity comparison} show the results of the experiment where the angled glass was positioned in a linear slider with a fixed magnification and only the defocus position was changed. Figure \ref{fig: S vs l slider experiment} plots the resulting sensitivity varied with defocus distance. The points are symmetric around the Y-axis, which means that the mechanism that makes a negative defocus configuration more powerful than its positive counterpart is not the negative distance per se but the increased magnification that comes with a shorter focal distance to the camera. Although the resolution has not been measured in this case, it is also to be expected through Equation \ref{equation: S over smin} that the spatial resolution trade-off is also improved by this increase in magnification.

\subsection{Uncertainty of the Angled Glass Experiment}

\par An uncertainty budget is required before any disagreement in the experiment can be interpreted. The campaign compares a displacement predicted by Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass} against a displacement measured by cross-correlation and each side carries its own uncertainty. So two budget were constructed based on the Guide to the Expression of uncertainty in Measurement (GUM).

The first budget focus in the three inputs of the angled glass displacement prediction, namely the plate thickness $T$, the plate angle $\theta$ and the refractive index $n$. Thickness was measured with a caliper and the angle was set with a protractor, while refractive index was obtained in literature as the value for common glass. Each input was treated as a rectangular probability distribution of half-width $a$ with a standard uncertainty of $u = a/\sqrt{3}$. Table \ref{tab: angled glass uncertainty} displays the half-width $a$ and uncertainty $u$ for each input variable. 

The sensitivity coefficients and contributions of each input variable regarding the two glass angles were also estimated based on Equation \ref{equation: Displacement of angled glass}. 
Table \ref{tab: angled glass uncertainty} shows the contribution of each variable for each glass angle, which correspond to the sensitivity coefficient times the uncertainty. At the bottom of the table it is also possible to note the combined uncertainties for each angle.
