\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chapter: Conclusion}

This thesis investigated the implementation of Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren in a supersonic wind tunnel. The method was approached through four experimental campaigns of increasing complexity that tackle each of its three main components: the optical configuration, the speckle generator and the refractive object. First, Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} presented a design methodology developed to balance the sensitivity and spatial resolution of a SBOS setup to reach an optimal physical layout. This methodology was applied, tested and refined to design the experimental setups described in Chapter \ref{chapter: Experimental Setups}. Different optical configurations were tested to image the distortions produced by a simple refractive object, which allowed for the validation of the methodology and comparison of the various setups' performance parameters. A dedicated campaign investigated the production of speckle patterns, arriving at a configuration capable of producing speckles with average diameters between 2--5 px. Then, an under-expanded compressible jet served as a test subject for the quantitative density reconstruction of a real schlieren object using displacement fields obtained through SBOS. The final experimental campaign used the method to study the flow of a linear aerospike nozzle in a high-speed wind tunnel environment. The results gathered and discussed in Chapter \ref{chapter: Results} make it possible to answer the main research question and the five sub-questions posed at the start of this thesis in Chapter \ref{chapter: introduction}.

\section{Practical Guidelines for the Design of a SBOS Setup}
\label{section: practical Guidelines for the Design of SBOS}

The first research sub-question intended to discover what should be the practical guidelines for designing a SBOS setup. Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} partially answered this question by summarizing the design guidelines resulting from the analysis of the design space surface maps. These recommendations survived the implementation in real experimental setups. However, further recommendations were collected throughout the duration of the thesis and can now be presented as a whole to aid the implementation of this technique by other researchers.

Regarding the \textbf{optical setup}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The equations collected here and the surface maps constructed by solving them display the main trends of a SBOS optical setup. These serve as useful tools for preliminary design, as has been validated through its implementation in the four experimental campaigns.

    \item A negative defocus configuration is preferable to positive defocus. It provides a higher magnification for the same defocus distance, this way improving the Sensitivity-Resolution trade-off presented in Equation \ref{equation: S over smin}.

    \item A higher $f_\#$ is preferable as it makes a better sensitivity-resolution trade-off easier to achieve. However, the smaller aperture might reduce contrast and require larger exposure times, which increase motion blur. SBOS uses a laser directly pointed at the camera, which produces high luminous contrast, allowing for very small exposure times.

    \item A longer focal length tends to make the setup more robust. A longer $f$ widens the spacing between isolines in the design space, so the performance parameters become less sensitive to positioning errors during assembly. It also raises the sensitivity ceiling of a positive defocus setup, given by Equation \ref{equation: BOS Sensitivity limit}. A moderately shorter lens can be adopted when the physical size of the setup is constrained, at little cost in sensitivity and spatial resolution.

    \item The SR criterion can be used to match the optical and processing resolutions. Equation \ref{equation: SR} allows the selection of how many $s_{min}$ could be imaged for a certain field of view. Equation \ref{equation: smin px} also expresses the minimum distinguishable feature in pixels, which allows the interrogation window to be selected so neither the optics nor the software resolution limits the measurements.

    \item The use of a commercially available compound camera lens led to a shift in the distances prescribed by the methodology. The optical magnification should, therefore, be taken from a calibration target at the in-focus plane rather than from the design values.
\end{itemize}

Regarding the \textbf{speckle generator}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A low power laser is sufficient for SBOS measurements. The 0.5 mW class 2 continuous laser used in this work produced a pattern of adequate contrast and speckle pattern. The high luminous contrast also permitted short exposure times.

    \item The diffuser and the beam should be sized to fill the field of view. The diffuser should be large enough to cover the desired object field of view, and the beam should be expanded to illuminate it. A beam that is too narrow becomes diffraction limited at the diffuser rather than at the lens pupil, which enlarges the speckle and removes its dependence on the aperture. This was probably the cause of the oversized speckles of the first campaign, where a diffuser of 1.27 cm was used with an unexpanded beam, as it was discussed in Section \ref{subsection: Speckle Beam Diameter}.

    \item Ideally the speckle size should stay between 2--5 pixels. This value comes from the literature review of Chapter \ref{chapter: LiteratureReview} and is the optimal size to avoid peak locking and not degrading the three-point Gaussian fit. Nevertheless, a larger speckle size can be used, given that a corresponding larger interrogation window is used, thus degrading the spatial resolution.

    \item The f-stop can be used to tune the speckle size. Section \ref{subsection: Speckle Aperture and Magnification} showed that measured speckle size followed the aperture as expected, while the optical magnification sweep produced almost no change. With a compound camera lens the aperture appears to be the only reliable handle on the speckle size.

    \item The speckle generator should be ideally isolated from the flow. Any movement of the diffuser displaces the whole pattern, which introduces shifts that may lead to decorrelation.

    \item Both projected and directed speckles can be used. Both methods produced patterns of comparable size and deviation, so the choice depends on practical considerations. Projected speckles suit applications with a single optical access or a laser powerful enough to damage the sensor, while directed speckles provide more intensity.

\end{itemize}

Regarding the \textbf{refractive object:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The strength of the density gradients can lead to excessive blur that must be counteracted with a smaller defocus. In a flow with strong gradients and considerable turbulence, the defocused configurations blurred the speckle pattern to the point of decorrelation. Consequently, the resulting displacement fields were dominated by noise, as shown in Figure \ref{fig: aerospike displacement points 1-3}. Moving the in-focus plane closer to the test section, or even inside of it, proved effective in reducing the blur and made it possible to image the flow.

    \item The thickness of the test section should be taken into account when designing the setup. The design methodology presented in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} was limited to an infinitely thin test section to define the spatial resolution and sensitivity. However, the thick wind-tunnel test section increased the effective defocus distance, which worsened considerably the $s_{min}$ figure.

\end{itemize}

The guidelines presented in this section give a concise set of recommendations on how to design an SBOS experimental setup. They summarize all that has been learned throughout this thesis on the design aspect of the method. With them, an optimal optical configuration and speckle generation arrangement can be reached for various applications. The next section will address how SBOS compares with the different optical density measurement techniques tested for this work.

\section{SBOS Compared to Other Techniques}
\label{section: SBOS versus other techniques}

This work sought to answer how SBOS compares to traditional BOS. The main difference is the ability to dynamically modify the reference position by adjusting the in-focus distance. The design methodology already noticed the advantage, when it was identified that negative defocus provided a better sensitivity-resolution ratio than positive defocus. The angled glass experiment validated this claim by comparing multiple optical configurations. Later, different defocus configurations were also tested during the high-speed wind tunnel experiment, which also allowed the method to be compared with traditional schlieren. Together, these experiments allow us to answer the second research sub-question and see how the performance of SBOS setups fares against traditional techniques.

The design methodology and angled glass experiment identified the mechanism that makes negative defocus preferable to the standard BOS setup. SBOS provides lower focal distances, leading to higher accessible magnification. The magnification increases the sensitivity-resolution ratio presented in Equation \ref{equation: S over smin}, meaning that a higher resolution is possible with little loss in sensitivity. Moreover, not using a physical background allows for the reference to be positioned much closer to the test section and even inside the test section, which was tested in the final experiment.

The wind tunnel experiment imaged the flow at a fixed camera position, varying the in-focus position to be in front of, inside and behind the test section. The negative defocus presented a higher sensitivity than positive defocus, with the inside position presenting the lowest sensitivity. Without the freestream flow, both defocus configurations presented spurious blur that made no structures distinguishable, while with the Mach 2 freestream the negative defocus blurred considerably less and already revealed part of the flow structures. The position inside the test section showed increased spatial resolution, which allowed the qualitative study of the flow. This demonstrates another advantage of SBOS: the capability to dynamically change the defocus position to increase the resolution or sensitivity on demand.

Traditional schlieren was also compared to the results obtained with SBOS during the wind-tunnel experiment. In-focus SBOS resolved the compression waves, expansion fans and shear layer identified in the schlieren images of the same flow condition, which allowed the same qualitative interpretation of the flow structures. The comparison was qualitative and restricted to the conditions of Section \ref{subsection: Aerospike Freestream Results}, so it indicates that in-focus SBOS can approach the resolution of schlieren in this configuration rather than establishing an equivalence between the two techniques. Additionally, SBOS poses certain advantages over schlieren. The setup is considerably simpler, needing fewer and smaller elements than schlieren. Furthermore, the displacement field obtained can more easily be turned into a quantitative density field than schlieren.

The evidence collected during this thesis shows how SBOS compares to traditional techniques. Its unique capability of dynamically changing the in-focus plane allowed it to approach the resolution of schlieren in the wind tunnel campaign and to reach sensitivities beyond what standard BOS permits, since a physical background cannot be placed as close to the test section. Moreover, its high luminous background allows for smaller exposure times, making it suitable for high-speed flows. The method's increased resolution and sensitivity also permits the imaging of small fields of view that would be unfeasible through standard BOS.

\section{In-Focus SBOS and the S ratio method}
\label{section: In-focus SBOS}

Defocus is an inherent part of BOS, meaning that worse spatial resolution than techniques that focus on the test section, such as schlieren and interferometry, is inevitable. To avoid the blur dominating the image, BOS has been mainly applied to large scale applications. The need for a physical background and the dependence of the sensitivity to the defocus distance never permitted the method to have zero defocus until SBOS emerged. Indeed, in-focus SBOS has been tried before by Meier and Rösgen with their double-pass technique \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. However, their technique derived an equation that could survive a zero defocus distance $l = 0$. Focusing inside the test section using a simple directed or projected speckle method that retrieves the sensitivity as $S = l_{eff}\cdot M_{ag}$ was not found in the literature reviewed in Chapter \ref{chapter: LiteratureReview}, which is unsurprising since $l = 0$ would lead to $S = 0$, implying that no displacement would be measured.

Displacements with focus inside the test section can in fact be measured, given the thickness of the test section. However, they do not follow the $S = l_{eff} \cdot M_{ag}$ model and a different method to retrieve the sensitivity must be used. This work presented a simple way to compute this quantity through the ratio of the displacements of the same flow condition taken with two distinct sensitivities. Since DaVis computes the in-plane displacement using the magnification calibration, $l_{eff}$ was computed through the ratio of sensitivity and magnification $l_{eff} = S/M_{ag}$ to obtain the density gradients through Equation \ref{equation: In-plane displacement}. The same process was done with two independent points, which agreed within 5\%.

The effective lever arms recovered in this way deserve a remark, since they do not follow the geometric model. For the point imaged without the freestream flow, $l + G + W/2$ is zero, so the model predicts no sensitivity and therefore no displacement at all, while the ratio method of Table \ref{tab: S ratio} returned a sensitivity corresponding to $l_{eff} = 126.29$ mm against a measured displacement of 14.96 px. For the points imaged with the freestream flow, the geometry listed in Table \ref{tab: aerospike test matrix} gives $l_{eff} = 90$ mm, whereas the ratio method of Table \ref{tab: S ratio points 4 to 11} returned 123.5 mm. The two configurations differ by 90 mm in defocus distance and still converge to nearly the same lever arm, which indicates that once the in-focus plane lies inside the test section the effective lever arm stops following $l$ and appears to be set by the thickness of the test section and its windows instead. The mechanism behind this behavior was not investigated in this work and remains an open question.

As a consequence of using in-focus SBOS, blur dropped sharply and produced a much clearer displacement field. Nevertheless, the sensitivity was lower and the method's accuracy was dependent on a second measurement where the flow's conditions must be as similar as possible to the in-focus conditions. One solution could be the simultaneous imaging of the flow. This could be done with the use of a split diopter lens, which allows the capture of two different magnifications in the same image, or two cameras imaging the same flow simultaneously set at different focus. All in all, in-focus SBOS provides high spatial resolution and similar ease for quantitative measurements as BOS, but adds a complication to the image capturing procedure. The last two sections addressed the advantages of the SBOS method, by comparing it to other methods and presenting the potential of this new in-focus SBOS. Now, the limitations and the uncertainties associated with SBOS will be discussed.

\section{Uncertainties and Limitations of SBOS}
\label{section: Uncertainties and Limitations}

This thesis had the care to identify and quantify the main sources of uncertainties of the methods applied, together with validation and verification of the results obtained. The design methodology was validated against the sensitivity values of various optical setups in the angled glass campaign. Furthermore, the same campaign included uncertainty budgets of the measured displacements and sensitivity. Similar budgets were also built for the autocorrelation method used to compute the average speckle size in the speckle pattern investigation. In the compressible jet experiment, a verification of the $NPR$ estimate led to notable agreement with the $L_s$ data of Hartmann and Lazarus \cite{Hartmann1941}. The quantitative density field obtained through Abel inversion with Tikhonov regularization presented a close match to the first cell's maximum and minimum of a compressible jet with the same $M_j$ but different Re, which showed the capability of SBOS to produce accurate quantitative measurements. In the wind tunnel experiment, the data obtained was validated through its comparison with schlieren images of the same flow condition. Later, the proposed method to compute the sensitivity of in-focus SBOS through the ratio of the displacements was verified by the comparison of the result through two independent routes.

The density gradient fields obtained through the measured displacements faced uncertainties mainly from the vector field scatter resultant of the cross-correlation technique and the defocus distance measurement. This becomes evident through the uncertainty budget shown in Table \ref{tab: measurement uncertainty}, where the contribution of the cross-correlation to the uncertainty ranged from 2.01\% to 6.04\% considering the deviations of Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results}. Both bounds correspond to the same quantity, the relative scatter $\sigma_\Delta/\Delta_x$, and its spread is a consequence of the optical configuration rather than of the method itself. Separating the two campaigns shows this clearly, as the nine points that varied the f-stop, focal length and defocus distance cover the whole range, while the eleven points of the slider experiment shared a single configuration and remained between 2.56\% and 2.89\%. The mean displacement proved considerably more stable than the individual vectors, since those same eleven points of Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} measured one displacement eleven times with a standard deviation of 0.35\%, which indicates that a large part of the vector scatter averages out over the field. The contribution of the defocus distance $l$ is also presented in Table \ref{tab: measurement uncertainty} spanning from 0.38\% to 5.77\%. It is also possible to understand both as clear sources of uncertainties through Equation \ref{equation: In-plane displacement} where only $l_{eff}$ and $\Delta \xi$ are truly dependent on the method implementation compared to the Gladstone-Dale constant and the reference refractive index $n_0$. The error associated with $l$ is mitigated by the precise measurement of the distance, while the CC scatter can be dealt with by reducing blur and avoiding decorrelation.

Meier and Rösgen \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013} and Bühlmann \cite{Buhlmann2020} had already cited decorrelation as one of the main drawbacks of SBOS. This effect can be caused by vibrations in the speckle generator, which move the diffuser relative to the laser and change the speckled pattern, or by the flow itself, as reported by Bühlmann and experienced here in the wind-tunnel, where deformation of the speckled image by strong gradients causes the cross-correlation to fail. The recurrence of the effect across independent setups is less telling than its origin, since decorrelation follows from the interferometric nature of the speckle pattern itself. Any change in the optical path, whether produced by a movement of the diffuser or by a gradient that deforms the wavefront, modifies the pattern instead of merely displacing it, so the reference and the flow image cease to be the same pattern. This is why the effect appears in projected and directed setups alike and should be treated as intrinsic to the method rather than as a failure of a particular implementation. However, the effect can be mitigated by certain measures. During the compressible jet and wind-tunnel campaign, extra care was taken to isolate the speckle pattern generator from the refractive object, therefore mitigating decorrelation induced by vibration. Moreover, reducing the blur by lowering the defocus proved an effective method to combat the decorrelation induced by it. This effect must then be taken into consideration when designing the experimental setup.


Another limitation was experienced during the speckle pattern investigation, when the attempt to replicate the Rayleigh limit of Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit} for average speckle sizes failed to note an effect of the optical magnification. The insensitivity of the speckle size was attributed to the use of a commercial compound lens because the introduction of the magnification in the equation derives from the use of the thin lens equation. Further investigation on the topic would clarify the true reason why this insensitivity occurred and possibly better clarify the mechanisms that control speckle formation. A better understanding of the speckle might be particularly useful for applications with very small scales as it would allow for finer manipulation of its size.

The verifications and validations done throughout this thesis uphold the relevance of the methods tested and results obtained. The design methodology was validated against various optical configurations with reasonably low deviations that could be explained if otherwise. The same can be said for the compressible jet, where the $NPR$ estimates were scrutinized against literature and survived. Moreover, the density field was computed and compared against a jet with a similar flow condition, matching the first peak within less than 1\% of deviation and the valley within less than 5\%. The $S$ ratio method also displayed promising results, with deviations of less than 1.5 mm.

This section concerned the verity and bounds of the conclusions by reiterating the uncertainties, limitations and the validity of the results. Additionally, the advantages of SBOS and how it fares against different techniques was discussed in the last section. The next section will give the final considerations of the method, contrasting strengths and weaknesses side-by-side, ending it with what could be improved and future applications.
