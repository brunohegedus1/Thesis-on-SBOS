\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chapter: Conclusion}

This thesis investigated the implementation of Laser-Speckled Background Oriented Schlieren, with final validation provided by a supersonic wind tunnel experiment. The method was approached through four experimental campaigns of increasing complexity that tackle each of its three main components: the optical configuration, the speckle generator and the refractive object. First, Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} presented a design methodology developed to balance the sensitivity and spatial resolution of an SBOS setup to reach an optimal physical layout. This methodology was applied, tested and refined to design the experimental setups described in Chapter \ref{chapter: Experimental Setups}. Different optical configurations were tested to image the distortions produced by a simple refractive object, which allowed for the validation of the geometric predictions of the methodology, namely the magnification and the displacement, and the comparison of the various setups' performance parameters. A dedicated campaign investigated the production of speckle patterns, arriving at a configuration capable of producing speckles with average diameters between 2--5 px. Then, an under-expanded compressible jet served as a test subject for the quantitative density reconstruction of a real refractive object using displacement fields obtained through SBOS. The final experimental campaign used the method to study the flow of a linear aerospike nozzle in a high-speed wind tunnel environment. The results gathered and discussed in Chapter \ref{chapter: Results} make it possible to answer the main research question and the five sub-questions posed at the start of this thesis in Chapter \ref{chapter: introduction}.

\section{Practical Guidelines for the Design of an SBOS Setup}
\label{section: practical Guidelines for the Design of SBOS}

The first research sub-question intended to discover what should be the practical guidelines for designing an SBOS setup, including its optical configuration and speckle generation. Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} partially answered this question by summarizing the design guidelines resulting from the analysis of the design space surface maps. These recommendations held when implemented in the experimental setups. However, further recommendations were collected throughout the duration of the thesis and can now be presented as a whole to aid the implementation of this technique by other researchers.

Regarding the \textbf{optical setup}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The equations of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} and the surface maps constructed by solving them display the main trends of an SBOS optical setup. These serve as useful tools for preliminary design, as shown by their use in the four experimental campaigns.

    \item Where the density gradients are moderate, a negative defocus configuration is preferable to positive defocus. It provides a higher magnification for the same defocus distance, this way improving the sensitivity-resolution trade-off presented in Equation \ref{equation: S over smin}. Where the gradients are strong enough to blur the pattern to decorrelation, the in-focus plane should instead be moved into the test section, as discussed below.

    \item A higher $f_\#$ is preferable as it makes a better sensitivity-resolution trade-off easier to achieve. The f-stop is, however, bounded by the speckle size, which grows with it: keeping the speckle within 2--5 pixels confines the achievable $S/s_{min}$ to a fixed interval if the speckle follows the Rayleigh limit (Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology}). Since the measured speckle grew more weakly with the f-stop than predicted, the actual coupling is looser, but it still sets a bound. Moreover, the smaller aperture might reduce contrast and require larger exposure times, which increase motion blur. Directed SBOS uses a laser pointed at the camera, which produces high luminous contrast, allowing exposure times of a few hundred microseconds with a 0.5 mW laser.

    \item A longer focal length tends to make the setup more robust. A longer $f$ widens the spacing between isolines in the design space, so the performance parameters become less sensitive to positioning errors during assembly. It also raises the sensitivity ceiling of a positive defocus setup, given by Equation \ref{equation: BOS Sensitivity limit}. A moderately shorter lens can be adopted when the physical size of the setup is constrained, at little cost in sensitivity and spatial resolution.

    \item The SR criterion can be used to match the optical and processing resolutions. Equation \ref{equation: SR} allows the selection of how many $s_{min}$ could be imaged for a certain field of view. Equation \ref{equation: smin px} also expresses the minimum distinguishable feature in pixels, which allows the interrogation window to be selected so neither the optics nor the software resolution limits the measurements.

    \item The use of a commercially available compound camera lens led to a shift in the distances prescribed by the methodology. The optical magnification should, therefore, be taken from a calibration target at the in-focus plane rather than from the design values.
\end{itemize}

Regarding the \textbf{speckle generator}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A low power laser is sufficient for SBOS measurements. The 0.5 mW class 2 continuous laser used in this work produced a pattern of adequate contrast and speckle size.

    \item The diffuser and the beam should be sized to fill the field of view. The diffuser should be large enough to cover the desired object field of view, and the beam should be expanded to illuminate it. A beam that is too narrow becomes diffraction limited at the diffuser rather than at the lens pupil, which enlarges the speckle and removes its dependence on the aperture. This was probably the cause of the oversized speckles of the first campaign, where the beam, although collimated to fill the diffuser, was limited to its 1.27 cm diameter, as it was discussed in Section \ref{subsection: Speckle Beam Diameter}.

    \item Ideally, for cross-correlation, the speckle size should stay between 2--5 pixels. This value comes from the literature review of Chapter \ref{chapter: LiteratureReview} and is the optimal size to avoid peak locking and not degrading the three-point Gaussian fit. Nevertheless, a larger speckle size can be used, given that a corresponding larger interrogation window is used, thus degrading the spatial resolution.

    \item The f-stop can be used to tune the speckle size. Section \ref{subsection: Speckle Aperture and Magnification} showed that the measured speckle size increased with the f-stop, although by a factor of only about 1.4 when it was doubled instead of the predicted 2, while the optical magnification sweep produced almost no change. With a compound camera lens the aperture appears to be the only reliable handle on the speckle size.

    \item The speckle generator should ideally be isolated from the flow and from the structure that carries it. A rigid in-plane translation of the diffuser only shifts the pattern, and that shift can be measured and removed. Deformation, tilt and out-of-plane motion change the pattern instead of displacing it, so the reference and the flow image cease to be the same pattern, which leads to decorrelation. Two optical accesses make the separation straightforward, since the generator can sit outside the test section. With a single optical access this may be unavoidable, and the residual shift must be corrected.

    \item Both projected and directed speckles can be used. Both methods produced patterns of comparable size and deviation, so the choice depends on practical considerations. Projected speckles suit applications with a single optical access or a laser powerful enough to damage the sensor, while directed speckles are expected to provide more intensity

\end{itemize}

Regarding the \textbf{refractive object}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The strength of the density gradients can lead to excessive blur that must be counteracted with a smaller defocus. In a flow with strong gradients and considerable turbulence, the defocused configurations blurred the speckle pattern to the point of decorrelation. Consequently, the resulting displacement fields were dominated by noise, as shown in Figure \ref{fig: aerospike displacement points 1-3}. Moving the in-focus plane closer to the test section, or even inside of it, proved effective in reducing the blur and made it possible to image the flow. In that case, the sensitivity no longer follows $S = l_{eff} M_{ag}$ and must be recovered from a second measurement of the same flow through the $S$ ratio method (Section \ref{section: In-focus SBOS}).

    \item The thickness of the test section should be taken into account when designing the setup. The design methodology presented in Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} was limited to an infinitely thin test section to define the spatial resolution and sensitivity. However, the thick wind-tunnel test section increased the effective defocus distance, which worsened considerably the $s_{min}$ figure.

\end{itemize}

The guidelines presented in this section give a concise set of recommendations on how to design an SBOS experimental setup. They summarize what has been learned throughout this thesis on the design aspect of the method. With them, a suitable optical configuration and speckle generation arrangement can be reached for various applications. The next section will address how SBOS compares with the other optical density measurement techniques considered in this work.

\section{SBOS Compared to Other Techniques}
\label{section: SBOS versus other techniques}

The second research sub-question asked how SBOS compares in sensitivity and spatial resolution to standard BOS and traditional schlieren. The main difference from BOS is the ability to dynamically modify the reference position by adjusting the in-focus distance. The question was answered through the design methodology, the angled glass experiment and the high-speed wind tunnel experiment, which also allowed the method to be compared with traditional schlieren.

The design methodology and angled glass experiment identified the mechanism that makes negative defocus preferable to the standard BOS setup. Negative defocus places the in-focus plane between the camera and the refractive object, shortening $d_f$ and leading to a higher accessible magnification. The magnification increases the sensitivity-resolution ratio presented in Equation \ref{equation: S over smin}, meaning that a higher resolution is possible with little loss in sensitivity. Moreover, not using a physical background allows for the reference to be positioned much closer to the test section and even inside the test section, which was tested in the final experiment. In the angled glass experiment, negative defocus reached sensitivities 46 to 56\% higher than positive defocus at the same defocus distance and field of view, and 27\% higher in the wind tunnel. The corresponding gain in spatial resolution follows from Equation \ref{equation: S over smin} but was not measured directly. Additionally, the speckle pattern investigation showed that the laser speckle is finer, denser and of higher contrast than the printed background available in the laboratory, and that its size can be tuned through the lens aperture. This allows small interrogation windows, and therefore a finer processing resolution, without manufacturing a new background for each setup.

The wind tunnel experiment imaged the flow at a fixed camera position for each set of points, varying the in-focus position to be in front of, inside and behind the test section. The negative defocus presented a higher sensitivity than positive defocus, with the inside position presenting the lowest sensitivity. Without the freestream flow, both defocus configurations presented spurious blur that made no structures distinguishable, while with the Mach 2 freestream the negative defocus blurred considerably less and already revealed part of the flow structures. The position inside the test section produced a visibly sharper displacement field, which allowed the qualitative study of the flow. This demonstrates another advantage of SBOS: the capability to dynamically change the defocus position to increase the resolution trading sensitivity and vice-versa on demand.

Traditional schlieren was also compared to the results obtained with SBOS during the wind-tunnel experiment. In-focus SBOS resolved the compression waves, expansion fans and shear layer identified in the schlieren images of the same flow condition, which allowed the same qualitative interpretation of the flow structures. The comparison was qualitative and restricted to the conditions of Section \ref{subsection: Aerospike Freestream Results}, so it indicates that in-focus SBOS can approach the resolution of schlieren in this configuration rather than establishing an equivalence between the two techniques. Additionally, SBOS offers certain advantages over schlieren. The setup is considerably simpler, needing fewer and smaller elements than schlieren. Furthermore, the displacement field obtained can more easily be turned into a quantitative density field than a schlieren intensity image.

The evidence collected during this thesis shows how SBOS compares to traditional techniques. Its unique capability of dynamically changing the in-focus plane allowed it to approach the resolution of schlieren in the wind tunnel campaign and to reach a higher sensitivity than standard BOS at the same defocus distance and field of view. Moreover, its bright background allowed exposures of a few hundred microseconds with a low-power laser. This is sufficient for steady or time-averaged high-speed flows, while resolving unsteady structures would require a pulsed or more powerful source. The method's increased resolution and sensitivity also make small fields of view more easily accessible than with standard BOS. The next section treats the configuration that none of these techniques allows, with the in-focus plane placed inside the test section itself.

\section{SBOS with In-Focus Schlieren Object and the S Ratio Method}
\label{section: In-focus SBOS}

Defocus is an inherent part of BOS, meaning that worse spatial resolution than techniques that focus on the test section, such as schlieren and interferometry, is inevitable. To avoid the blur dominating the image, BOS has been mainly applied to large scale applications. The need for a physical background and the dependence of the sensitivity on the defocus distance never permitted the method to have zero defocus until SBOS emerged. Indeed, in-focus SBOS has been tried before by Meier and Rösgen with their double-pass technique \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013}. They developed a technique, where the derived equation for sensitivity was not zero when the defocus distance was null $l = 0$. No instance was found in the literature reviewed in Chapter \ref{chapter: LiteratureReview} of SBOS being used with the focus inside the test section through single-pass projected or directed speckles, where the sensitivity is retrieved as $S = l_{eff}\cdot M_{ag}$. Since $l = 0$, that would lead to $S = 0$ and imply that no displacement could be measured.

Nevertheless, displacements with the focus at or inside the refractive object can in fact be measured. This was first observed at point 10 of the angled glass experiment, where the plate lay in the in-focus plane and still produced a displacement of 29.37 px, and later in the wind tunnel. However, they do not follow the $S = l_{eff} \cdot M_{ag}$ model and a different method to retrieve the sensitivity must be used. This work presented a simple way to compute this quantity through the ratio of the displacements of the same flow condition taken with two distinct sensitivities. Since DaVis computes the in-plane displacement using the magnification calibration, $l_{eff}$ was computed through the ratio of sensitivity and magnification $l_{eff} = S/M_{ag}$ to obtain the density gradients through Equation \ref{equation: In-plane displacement}. The two estimates of the in-focus sensitivity differed by 4.6\%. Moreover, the discrepancy observed between the $l_{eff}$ obtained through this method and the usual formulation for defocus points $l_{eff} = l+G+W/2$ further demonstrates that the previous model will break for references inside the test section.

As a consequence of using in-focus SBOS, blur dropped sharply and produced a much clearer displacement field. Nevertheless, the sensitivity was lower and the method's accuracy was dependent on a second measurement where the flow's conditions must be as similar as possible to the in-focus conditions. One solution could be the simultaneous imaging of the flow. This could be done with the use of a split diopter lens, which allows the capture of two different magnifications in the same image, or two cameras imaging the same flow simultaneously set at different focus. All in all, in-focus SBOS provides high spatial resolution and similar ease for quantitative measurements as BOS, but adds a complication to the image capturing procedure. The last two sections have addressed the advantages of the SBOS method, by comparing it to other methods and presenting the potential of this new in-focus SBOS. The next section addresses the challenges of applying SBOS in a high-speed wind tunnel.

\section{Implementing SBOS in a High-Speed Wind Tunnel}
\label{section: Wind Tunnel Implementation}

The fourth research sub-question asked which challenges and limitations arise when SBOS is applied in a high-speed wind tunnel to visualize the flow of a linear aerospike. The aerospike campaign identified four. First, the compound camera lens shifted the distances prescribed by the design methodology, so the magnification had to be measured on site and the setup adjusted to reach the desired field of view. Second, the thick test section and its windows increased the effective lever arm, raising the minimum distinguishable feature from the 10 px of the design to about 20 px for the defocused configurations. Third, the strong density gradients and turbulence of the nozzle exhaust blurred the speckle pattern of the defocused configurations to the point of decorrelation, which made it necessary to move the in-focus plane into the test section. Fourth, with the focus inside the test section the sensitivity no longer follows $S = l_{eff} M_{ag}$ and had to be recovered from a second measurement through the $S$ ratio method of Section \ref{section: In-focus SBOS}. Despite these challenges, the campaign showed that SBOS can be installed around an existing wind tunnel with a low-power laser, a diffuser and a single camera, and that in-focus SBOS resolves the main wave structures of the aerospike flow. The uncertainties associated with these measurements and the remaining limitations of the method are discussed next.

\section{Uncertainties and Limitations of SBOS}
\label{section: Uncertainties and Limitations}

The fifth research sub-question concerned the dominant sources of uncertainty in SBOS measurements and how accurately they can be converted into a quantitative density field. This thesis had the care to identify and quantify the main sources of uncertainties of the methods applied, together with validation and verification of the results obtained. The angled glass campaign included uncertainty budgets of the measured displacements and sensitivity, and similar budgets were built for the autocorrelation method used to compute the average speckle size in the speckle pattern investigation.

The density gradient fields obtained with the measured displacements from the three experiments conducted in this work faced uncertainties mainly from the vector field scatter resultant of the cross-correlation technique and the defocus distance measurement. This becomes evident through the uncertainty budget shown in Table \ref{tab: measurement uncertainty} for the angled glass experiment, where the contribution of the cross-correlation to the uncertainty ranged from 2.01\% to 6.04\% considering the deviations of Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results}. Separating the two angled glass campaigns shows this clearly, as the nine points that varied the f-stop, focal length and defocus distance cover the whole range, while the eleven points of the slider experiment shared a single configuration and remained between 2.56\% and 2.89\%. The mean displacement proved considerably more stable than the individual vectors, since those same eleven points of Table \ref{tab: angled glass displacement results} measured one displacement eleven times with a standard deviation of 0.35\%, which indicates that a large part of the vector scatter averages out over the field. The contribution of the defocus distance $l$ is also presented in Table \ref{tab: measurement uncertainty} spanning from 0.38\% to 5.77\%. Equation \ref{equation: In-plane displacement} shows why these two dominate: $K$ and $n_0$ are well-known constants, so the uncertainty of the density gradient comes from the measured displacement $\Delta\xi$ and the lever arm $l_{eff}$, that is, from the cross-correlation scatter and the defocus distance. The error associated with $l$ is mitigated by the precise measurement of the distance, while the CC scatter can be dealt with by reducing blur and avoiding decorrelation.

Meier and Rösgen \cite{Meier&Roesgen2013} and Bühlmann \cite{Buhlmann2020} had already cited decorrelation as one of the main drawbacks of SBOS. This effect can be caused by vibrations in the speckle generator, which move the diffuser relative to the laser and change the speckled pattern, or by the flow itself, as reported by Bühlmann and experienced here in the wind-tunnel, where deformation of the speckled image by strong gradients causes the cross-correlation to fail. The recurrence of the effect across independent setups is less telling than its origin, since decorrelation follows from the interferometric nature of the speckle pattern itself. Any change in the optical path, whether produced by a movement of the diffuser or by a gradient that deforms the wavefront, modifies the pattern instead of merely displacing it, so the reference and the flow image cease to be the same pattern. This is why the effect appears in projected and directed setups alike and should be treated as intrinsic to the method rather than as a failure of a particular implementation. However, the effect can be mitigated by certain measures. During the compressible jet and wind-tunnel campaign, extra care was taken to isolate the speckle pattern generator from the refractive object, therefore mitigating decorrelation induced by vibration. Moreover, reducing the blur by lowering the defocus proved an effective method to combat the decorrelation induced by it. This effect must then be taken into consideration when designing the experimental setup.


Another limitation emerged in the speckle pattern investigation. The measured average speckle size departed from the Rayleigh limit of Equation \ref{equation: Rayleigh Limit}: it grew more weakly with the f-stop than predicted, by a factor of about 1.4 instead of 2 when the f-stop was doubled, and showed no measurable effect of the optical magnification. Both deviations were attributed to the commercial compound lens, since the magnification enters the equation through the thin lens approximation. Further investigation would clarify their true cause and the mechanisms that control speckle formation, which would be particularly useful for very small scales, where finer control of the speckle size is needed.

The verifications and validations done throughout this thesis uphold the relevance of the methods tested and results obtained. The geometric predictions of the design methodology, namely the magnification and the displacement, were validated against various optical configurations in the angled glass campaign with low deviations, and the larger ones could be explained. In the compressible jet experiment, the $NPR$ estimate was found consistent with the $L_s$ data of Hartmann and Lazarus \cite{Hartmann1941}. The density field obtained through Abel inversion with Tikhonov regularization matched the first peak within 1\% and the first valley within 5\% of a compressible jet with the same $M_j$ but a different Reynolds number \cite{Panda1999}, although the two diverged from the second shock cell onwards. SBOS can therefore deliver quantitative density data, so far verified over the first shock cell. In the wind tunnel experiment, the data obtained was qualitatively compared with schlieren images of the same flow condition. Finally, the $S$ ratio method proposed to compute the sensitivity of in-focus SBOS matched the geometric route within 5\% at the two defocused points, and its two estimates of the in-focus sensitivity differed by 4.6\%.

This section addressed the validity and bounds of the conclusions by revisiting the uncertainties, limitations and validation of the results. The next section answers the main research question, before the final considerations contrast the strengths and weaknesses of the method and discuss what could be improved and future applications.

\section{Systematic Implementation of SBOS}
\label{section: systematic implementation}

The main research question asked how SBOS can be systematically implemented to study compressible flows in a laboratory and in a wind tunnel. The five sub-questions answered above describe such a route, and it begins with the application rather than with the hardware. The field of view at the refractive object, the smallest feature that must be resolved and the deflection expected from the flow together fix the sensitivity and the spatial resolution required. With those set, the design space maps of Chapter \ref{chapter: methodology} and the SR criterion give the focal length, the f-stop number and the defocus distance that balance the two, while the effective lever arm accounts for the thickness of the test section and its windows. The speckle generator follows from the same field of view, since the diffuser and the expanded beam must cover it, and the f-stop is then used to bring the average speckle size into the 2 to 5 pixel range. Because a compound camera lens shifts the prescribed distances, the optical magnification should be measured on site with a calibration target rather than taken from the design.

The refractive object determines the last choice. Where the density gradients are moderate, a negative defocus configuration is preferable, as it reaches a higher magnification at a shorter focal distance and therefore a better sensitivity-resolution ratio than standard BOS. Where the gradients are strong enough to blur the pattern to decorrelation, the in-focus plane can be moved into the test section instead, at the cost of a sensitivity that must be recovered from the ratio of displacements measured at a second configuration. In either case the result should be quantified through an uncertainty budget and verified by imaging the same flow condition twice, since the scaling from displacement to density gradient is otherwise unchecked. This route was followed in four campaigns of increasing complexity, from a glass plate on an optical rail to a linear aerospike nozzle at Mach 2, which indicates that it holds across the range of scales and flow conditions studied in this work.

This work's limitations and the weaknesses collected in Table \ref{tab: SBOS strengths and weaknesses} highlight the main aspects of the method that could be improved. First, the average speckle size insensitivity to magnification, as well as its weaker-than-predicted dependence on the f-stop, has not been fully explained. Section \ref{subsection: Speckle Aperture and Magnification} attributed the deviation to the use of a commercial compound lens; however, that has not been proven nor investigated in this work. Further assessment of that dependency would be useful, as it provides another parameter to tune the speckle pattern quality. Furthermore, a deeper theoretical study of the sensitivity of in-focus SBOS could lead to a better route to estimate that performance parameter that does not involve a second measurement. More practical solutions that still use the $S$ ratio method can also be tested, as the use of a split diopter lens or a second camera simultaneously imaging the flow at a different sensitivity.

The strengths of the method also position it as an interesting measurement technique for various applications. The gain in resolution and sensitivity allows the method to be used in smaller scales than traditional BOS. Laser speckles have already been used to image blood flow as reported by Boas and Dunn \cite{Boas&Dunn2010} but it could also be used in microfluidic applications. This could be done by improving the work of Sun and Hsiao \cite{Sun&Hsiao2014}, who placed a lithographed photomask over a microscale channel. Replacing that photomask with a speckle pattern would remove the need for a background in contact with the chip, which is the constraint that currently sets the geometry of such measurements. Reactive flows are equally promising, as Michalski et al. \cite{Michalski2018} have already demonstrated that SBOS can be used for a light-emitting flow, when they studied the calorimetry of an electrical spark discharge. The narrow-band laser background can be separated from the flame emission with a simple filter. Additionally, a pulsed or higher-power laser would permit the short exposures that suit the unsteadiness of combustion. In all of these cases the guidelines gathered here will provide a starting point for the design of the setup.
